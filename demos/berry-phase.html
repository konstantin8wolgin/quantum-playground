A Phase From the Shape of the Path

A spin follows a magnetic field. The field turns slowly around a loop and comes back. The spin comes back too, but its arrow has turned by an extra angle. Part of this angle depends on how long the loop took. The other part does not. It depends only on the area the loop encloses on a sphere. This is the Berry phase.

How to use this room
  1. Part 1: press Play. The orange dot is the direction of the field. The blue arrow is the spin. Watch the blue arrow follow the orange dot around the loop. Drag the sphere to look from another side.
  2. Part 2: keep the loop slow (10 turns or more) and read the big number. It compares the simulated phase with the exact answer −Ω/2. Then move loop time to the left and watch the match break.
  3. Part 3: move the cone angle α. The geometric phase moves along a straight line. Then look at the table: speed, direction and spin state change the phase only in the way the area says.
  4. Part 4: look at the same phase as the flux of a tiny magnet (a monopole) through the loop. Compare it with the Aharonov–Bohm room.

Part 1 · The field draws a loop

A spin ½ sits in a magnetic field of fixed strength B. The direction of the field moves around a loop on a sphere. At the start the spin points along the field. Here the field turns slowly, so the spin follows it. The picture shows the Bloch sphere: the arrow is the direction of the spin (see the spinor flag room).

What the numbers mean

The field is B(t) = B·n(t), where n is a unit vector on the loop. The Hamiltonian is H = −(B/2) n·σ (ħ = 1). The spin along n has the energy −B/2. The spin against n has +B/2. The "fidelity" is |⟨+n(t)|ψ(t)⟩|², the chance to find the spin along the field now. It is 1 when the spin follows the field perfectly.

The "norm error" is the largest deviation of ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ from 1 during the run. Each step is an exact rotation (a unitary 2×2 matrix), so it stays near 1e-15.

Part 2 · Split the phase

After one loop the spin points along the field again. Its state has been multiplied by a phase eiΦ. Φ has two parts. The dynamical phase is the usual one: energy times time. For the aligned spin it is +BT/2 (E = −B/2). The rest is the geometric phase. For a slow loop it is −Ω/2, where Ω is the solid angle of the loop. For a cone, Ω = 2π(1 − cos α).

What the numbers mean

The big number uses its own run: T = 10 000/B, which is 1 600 spin turns. The loop is so slow that the leading error π²sin²α/(BT) is below 10⁻³ (for α = 60°). The line below it removes this error, and the rest is the error of the numerical method and the next order. The check "against the exact rotating frame" compares the amplitude of the run at the end with the closed formula. It uses the same T, so it is not tied to slow loops.

The phases are angles. Only Φ mod 2π means anything. So all differences are shown in the range −π to π.

Part 3 · Area, not time

The geometric phase is a straight line in the solid angle: γ = −Ω/2. The slope is −½. At α = 90° the loop is a great circle. Ω = 2π and γ = −π. The state is multiplied by −1. This is the same sign flip as in the spinor flag: a turn by 360° changes the sign of a spin.

What the numbers mean

For the loop shape you picked in Part 1, the area Ω is computed once from the loop. Each row then runs the Schrödinger equation with another speed profile or direction. The differences are small and shrink like 1/T. They are not exactly equal because the loop is slow but not infinitely slow. The "start–stop" row has a larger difference: its fastest part is faster, so it is less adiabatic.

Part 4 · The same idea as a magnetic flux

There is a second way to see −Ω/2. Imagine a tiny magnet with only one pole (a monopole) at the centre of the sphere. Its field points straight out. The flux of this field through a loop on the sphere is proportional to the area Ω. If the monopole has the strength ½, the flux is Ω/2. The geometric phase is minus this flux. This is the same rule as in the Aharonov–Bohm room: a phase equal to the flux through the loop.

What the numbers mean

The total flux of a monopole of strength ½ through the whole sphere is 4π·½ = 2π. A phase of 2π is invisible. This is why the strength must be a half-integer: the same fact as the spin being ½ (Dirac's rule). Moving the loop over the whole sphere changes the phase by exactly 2π and nothing else.

The Berry curvature F is the "magnetic field" of the Berry phase. For the aligned spin, F = −½ per unit solid angle. A loop gives γ = ∫F dΩ over the enclosed area. This uses Stokes' rule, as for a normal vector potential. The picture draws the monopole with the plus sign and writes the minus sign in front of its flux: phase = −flux.

Guide: what you are looking at

In one minute

Take a spin ½ (for example an electron or a neutron) in a magnetic field. The spin lines up with the field, like a compass needle. Now turn the direction of the field slowly around a loop and bring it back. The spin follows, and it points along the field again. Is it the same as before? Its arrow is. But the quantum state has gained a phase.

Part of this phase is the normal one: the state turns with the energy, and a longer loop gives a larger phase. The other part is strange. It does not depend on the time. It does not depend on the speed of the field. It depends only on the shape of the loop: the area that it encloses on a sphere. This extra phase is the Berry phase. It was found by Michael Berry in 1984, and it was measured soon after with light and with neutrons.

The result is a "memory of the path". The spin does not remember how fast the field moved. It remembers which way around the sphere the field went and how much area it enclosed.

Things to try

  1. Part 1: set a slow loop (30 spin turns) and press Play. Compare the arrow with the dot.
    What happens and whyThe blue arrow stays almost on the orange dot. The fidelity is close to 1. The field turns so slowly (it is adiabatic) that the spin has time to follow. This is the adiabatic case. The field direction changes little during one spin turn.
  2. Part 1: set a fast loop (0.3 turns).
    What happens and whyThe field direction moves more than the spin can follow in one turn. The spin lags behind and spins around the field. The fidelity falls. The state at the end is not the same direction as at the start, so the geometric phase is not clean any more.
  3. Part 2: read the big number at α = 60°. Then set α = 90°.
    What happens and whyAt 60° the loop encloses Ω = π. The phase is −π/2 (−1.5708). At 90° the loop is a great circle. Ω = 2π and the phase is −π (3.1416 in size). The state is multiplied by −1. The simulation agrees with these numbers to 1 part in 1000 or better. The small rest is the slow-loop error.
  4. Part 2: double the loop time and look at the dot in the right picture.
    What happens and whyThe distance from −Ω/2 becomes half as large. This is the 1/T law: the error is π²sin²α/(BT). It is the price of a finite speed. The geometric phase itself does not change.
  5. Part 2: make the loop very fast (0.1 turns).
    What happens and whyThe distance is not small. The spin does not follow the field, and the total phase has no simple split. A different split, found by Aharonov and Anandan, still gives a geometric phase for a fast loop. It is the area of the path of the spin (not of the field) on the sphere.
  6. Part 3: choose a loop shape other than the circle. Is the phase still on the line?
    What happens and whyYes. The tilted circle has the same area as the cone, so it has the same phase. The wobbly loop has another area, and the dot moves along the line to its new area. The phase follows the area, not the shape.
  7. Part 3: look at the table.
    What happens and whyA different speed along the loop gives the same phase (as the area), apart from a small difference from the finite loop time. The backwards loop gives the opposite sign: the area is now on the other side. The anti-aligned spin also has the opposite sign: its phase is +Ω/2.
  8. Part 4: switch the spin state and push α to 175°.
    What happens and whyFor the anti-aligned spin the monopole has the strength −½, and the radial arrows point inward. The phase changes sign. For α near 180° the loop shrinks to a point at the south pole, and the flux is nearly 2π (the whole sphere). The phase is nearly −2π. This is the same as no phase, because 2π cannot be seen.

The physics

A spin ½ in the field B n(t) has the Hamiltonian H = −(B/2) n·σ. Here σ are the Pauli matrices and ħ = 1. The two eigenstates are "along n" (energy −B/2) and "against n" (energy +B/2). If n changes slowly compared with the spin precession (one turn takes 2π/B), the Schrödinger equation keeps the spin in the same eigenstate. This is the adiabatic theorem.

After a closed loop the state is eiΦ times the start. The first part of Φ is the dynamical phase −∫E dt = +BT/2 for the aligned state. The second part is the Berry phase γ = −Ω/2. Berry's discovery was that γ does not depend on the speed at all. A different speed changes only T, and so only the dynamical part. The geometric part depends on the loop. It is the same under a change of the arbitrary phase of the eigenstate (a gauge change) because the loop is closed.

The Berry phase is a phase picked up by moving around a closed loop in a parameter space. It has the same form as the Aharonov–Bohm phase: the integral of a "vector potential" A (the vector potential of the spin) around the loop. By Stokes' rule it equals the flux of the "magnetic field" of this potential through the loop. For a spin ½ this field is that of a monopole of strength ½ at the centre of the sphere. The state space of a spin is an SU(2) story. The same sign flip appears in the spinor in the spinor flag room: for the great circle the Berry phase is −π, so the state gets a factor −1.

The field here is moved around a loop. In the experiments of Tomita and Chiao the same thing happened with light in a coiled fibre: the direction of the light's momentum drew a loop, and the polarisation turned by the Berry angle. Bitter and Dubbers saw it with neutrons in a twisted magnetic field. In both, the angle was set by the area of the loop on a sphere.

Honest limits

The spin sees a field of fixed size that is perfectly controlled. There is no noise, no relaxation, and the field has no quantum part. The "time" is in units where B = 1, so a loop of 10 turns is not a real time. The big number is a numerical result with a leading 1/T error (the error is below 10⁻³ for the default angle α = 60°, and up to 5·10⁻³ near α = 90°), not an infinite-time limit. The Aharonov–Anandan split for fast loops is named but not drawn. The tilted circle and the wobbly loop have no closed formula in this room: only the cone is compared with an exact solution. Their solid angle is a sum of small triangles (accurate to about 1e-6). The monopole is a picture in the space of field directions, not a real magnet. In a real lab (neutron beam, light in a fibre) the phase is measured with an interferometer, and the beam passes only once, so the loop is not repeated.
