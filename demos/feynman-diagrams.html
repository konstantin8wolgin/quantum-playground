Feynman Diagrams From a Path Integral

Where do the pictures come from? A toy theory with one number instead of a field. Its integral can be done exactly, so every claim here is checked. A Feynman diagram turns out to be a way to count.

How to use this room
  1. In part 1, press Pair the legs at random. Each press draws one diagram. Press Draw 1000 and compare the bars with the green marks.
  2. In part 2, move the g slider. Watch the error of the series fall, reach a smallest value, and then grow again.
  3. In part 3, see the first correction to the propagator. In part 4, see the same idea in real QED.

1. Diagrams are counting exactsimulation

The toy: Z(g) = ∫ dφ/√(2π) · exp(−φ²/2 − g φ⁴/4!). Expand the interaction in powers of g. The n-th term needs the average of φ4n in the plain Gaussian. This average is the number of ways to pair 4n legs: (4n−1)!! (Wick's theorem). Each pairing is a Feynman diagram.

Each card: the diagram, its symmetry factor S, and how many of the pairings give it. The bar is the share of your random draws. The green mark is the exact share (count / (4n−1)!!). The weight of a diagram is 1/S. Here 1/S = count / (n!·24n); the code also computes S from the symmetry formula and checks that both agree.

2. The series against the exact answer exact

Z(g) is one ordinary integral. The code does it by two quadratures (trapezoid and Simpson). The series Z = Σ aₙ gⁿ, aₙ = (−1/24)ⁿ (4n−1)!!/n!, is the sum of all vacuum diagrams. Cut it off after N terms and compare.

Squares: size of the term of order N. Line: error of the partial sum |ZN − Z|. Both on a log scale. The terms first shrink, then grow without limit. The dashed line marks the best cut-off.
Partial sum ZN against N. Dashed line: the exact Z(g). Points outside the box are shown as triangles at the edge.

Only connected diagrams count in ln Z exact

The diagrams that fall apart into separate pieces are products of smaller diagrams. They cancel in the logarithm. So ln Z = Σ cₙ gⁿ contains only connected vacuum diagrams. Check n = 2: the three diagrams weigh 1/128 + 1/16 + 1/48. Remove the separate pieces (1/128 = a₁²/2) and 1/16 + 1/48 = 1/12 is left, which is c₂.

Predict, then test: set g = 1. Will more terms give a better answer? Then set g = 0.01. How many terms are useful now? And what is the smallest error you can reach?

3. The propagator: ⟨φ²⟩ exact

The two-point function ⟨φ²⟩ = Z⁻¹ ∫ φ² e−S is the toy "propagator": a line from one end to the other. With g = 0 it equals 1. The first correction comes from one vertex with a loop on the line (the tadpole). Count it: two line ends and one vertex make 6 legs, which have 15 pairings. 3 pairings join the two ends to each other and leave a separate figure-eight. These cancel against Z. The other 12 pairings give the tadpole. Weight: −g · 12/4! = −g/2.

4. The bridge to real physics cartooninterpretation

In a real field theory the same book-keeping holds, with three changes. Each line is a propagator: the path integral of a free particle from one point to another (see Double Slit Flow for such a path sum). Each vertex is a coupling: the place where lines meet, with a strength. In QED the electron–photon vertex has strength about √α ≈ 0.085, where α ≈ 1/137. Each diagram is one term of the series, with a number for each line and each vertex. A loop is a closed circuit of lines: a free momentum that must be summed over. The QED series is also asymptotic, but α is small interpretation exact (known numbers)

Dyson argued in 1952 that the QED series in α cannot converge. The same thing happens in the toy: the terms grow like n!. But each new order brings only a factor of about α/π ≈ 1/430. The first terms give the magnetic moment of the electron, aₑ = (g−2)/2, to about eight digits. The table uses the published coefficients C₁ = 1/2, C₂ = −0.328479, C₃ = 1.181241, in aₑ = Σ Cₖ (α/π)ᵏ.

The remaining difference to the measurement is about 9·10⁻¹¹. It comes from the 4th-order and higher terms, which are not in this table. The table does not show the divergence: nobody has computed far enough, and a rough estimate puts the smallest term at hundreds of orders. The toy of part 2 shows what happens there.

Predict, then test: how many digits does the one-photon term α/2π get right? How many do the first two terms get right? Look at the table.

Guide: what you are looking at

In one minute

A Feynman diagram looks like a drawing of particles that move and collide. It is better to see it as a bookkeeping device. The path integral asks you to add up everything that can happen. When the particles interact, this sum is too hard to do exactly. So you expand it in powers of the coupling. Every term in the expansion is a counting problem: in how many ways can the lines be joined to the vertices? Each way is a diagram.

This room uses the smallest example, a "field" with only one value φ. The integral can be done exactly by a computer. So you can compare the series of diagrams with the true answer, and see what the series gets right and where it breaks. The break is real: the series does not converge, and the same is true for QED.

Things to try

  1. Set n = 1 and press "Pair the legs at random" several times.
    What happens and whyYou always get the figure-eight. There are only 3 ways to pair the four legs of one vertex, and all three give the same picture. The weight is 3/4! = 1/8, which is 1/S with S = 8. Two lines each can be turned round (2 × 2) and the two loops can be swapped (2).
  2. Set n = 2 and press "Draw 1000".
    What happens and whyOf the 105 pairings, 72 give the dumbbell (69 %), 24 give the sunset (23 %) and 9 give two separate figure-eights (8.6 %). The bars follow the green marks within about one percentage point. The weights 1/16, 1/48 and 1/128 add up to 105/1152, as they must.
  3. Set g = 1 in part 2 and look at plot A.
    What happens and whyThe terms shrink only for the first two orders. The best partial sum is Z₁ = 0.875 against the exact 0.918919, an error of about 0.044. Then the terms grow. The sum of 20 orders is wrong by about 8·10¹². The series is asymptotic: the coefficient aₙ grows like n!·(2/3)ⁿ-ish, faster than any power.
  4. Set g = 0.1.
    What happens and whyYou can use about 15 terms. The best cut-off, N = 14, has an error of about 2·10⁻⁸, the size of the smallest term. After that, the error grows. At g = 0.01 the best error is far below 10⁻¹⁵, below the rounding error of the computer. A smaller coupling gives a longer useful series and a much better answer. That is why QED, with α ≈ 1/137, works so well.
  5. Look at the ln Z table.
    What happens and whyThe coefficient c₂ = 1/12 is smaller than a₂ = 35/384. The difference is a₁²/2 = 1/128: the diagram made of two separate figure-eights. In ln Z all such separate products cancel. The connected weights for n = 1, 2, 3 are 1/8, 1/12 and 11/96, and the counting from the cards gives the same numbers.
  6. In part 3, set g = 0.1 and compare "1 − g/2" with the exact ⟨φ²⟩.
    What happens and whyThe exact value is 0.95558 and 1 − g/2 = 0.95. The error is 0.0056. It is close to the next term of the series, (2/3)g² = 0.0067, reduced a little by the term after it (−11g³/8 = −0.0014). The error scales like g², as it must when the first-order term is right.

The physics

In quantum field theory the path integral sums over all field configurations, with weight eiS/ħ (or e−S in imaginary time, as here; see imaginary time). The action S of a field has a free part, quadratic in the field, and an interaction part. The free part gives the propagator. The interaction part, expanded in powers of the coupling, gives perturbation theory.

The toy removes space and time. The field has one value φ. The propagator is a number: ⟨φ²⟩₀ = 1. Wick's theorem says that the Gaussian average of 2k copies of φ is the number of ways to pair them, (2k−1)!!. Draw each pairing as lines between the legs. This is a Feynman diagram. The factor 1/n! is for the order of the vertices, the factor 1/4! for the order of the legs. When we count diagrams as shapes, these factors turn into the symmetry factor 1/S.

In real physics each line also carries a momentum and a vertex has a rule, but the logic is the same. In QED the vertex joins two electron lines and one photon line. Its strength is the charge e, and e² = 4πα in natural units. A loop is a closed circuit of lines. Around a loop the momentum is free, and you must sum over it. Diagrams with loops make the vacuum act like a medium: the electron self-energy and the vacuum polarisation are such effects. The room Light takes every path shows the path sum for light itself; Every path is an arrow shows the sum for one particle.

Honest reading: a Feynman diagram is a term in a series. It is not a photograph of what happens. In the toy there is no time and no particle at all, yet the diagrams are exact terms. The pictures with "particles that fly and collide" are a way to remember the rules.

Honest limits

The toy has no space, no time and no particles. It has the counting of diagrams and the asymptotic series of QFT, but not the integrals over momenta, the infinities, or the renormalisation. The symmetry factors here are for vacuum diagrams of φ⁴ only. The three QED pictures are cartoons: they show the lines and vertices but carry no numbers. The statement that the QED series is asymptotic rests on Dyson's argument, which is a physical argument and not a proof. The g−2 table uses the published coefficients as input; this room does not compute them. A rough estimate puts the smallest QED term far beyond any computed order.
