Broken Symmetry

A field that likes to be "somewhere" on a circle, but has no reason to choose one place. Push it and see two kinds of motion. Then put such a field on every point of a grid, cool it down, and watch vortices freeze in.

What the numbers mean

Two motions on the hat. Up and down the wall the ball swings with frequency ω = 2μ: this is the Higgs mode (a massive particle). Along the valley nothing pushes back, so the frequency is zero: this is the Goldstone mode (a massless particle). Goldstone's theorem says: every broken continuous symmetry gives such a free direction. When μ² < 0 the hat becomes a bowl, nothing is broken, and both directions swing with the same frequency √(2|μ²|).

The grid. This is the XY model: energy E = −Σ cos(θi − θj) over neighbours, sampled with the Metropolis rule at temperature T. At low T the spins make long, slow waves: the Goldstone mode of the grid. Each wave holds an average energy of T/2, so the energy per site is close to −2 + T/2. Long waves fade slowly and short waves fade fast, because a long twist costs almost no energy.

Vortices. Around a vortex the angle turns by 360°. In its centre the field cannot pick an angle, so a real field must climb to the top of the hat there. Vortex and anti-vortex pairs bind at low T and come apart near TKT ≈ 0.893 (Kosterlitz and Thouless, Nobel Prize 2016). In two dimensions there is no true long-range order at any T > 0 (Mermin–Wagner), only this "quasi-order".

Cool-down. If you cool fast, distant regions pick their angles before they can talk to each other, and vortices get trapped where they meet. Slow cooling leaves fewer. This is the Kibble–Zurek idea, first proposed for the early universe and tested in superfluid helium and cold atoms.

Guide: what you are looking at

In one minute

Many things in nature "choose a direction" when they get cold. A magnet picks north or south. A superfluid picks a phase. Before the choice, all directions are equal. After the choice, one direction wins. Physicists call this symmetry breaking.

Part 1 shows the idea with one ball. The ball sits on a hill shaped like a Mexican hat. The lowest points of the hat form a circle: a valley. All points of the valley are equally good, but the ball must rest at one of them. It can move in two ways. It can go up the wall of the valley, and then it swings back. Or it can roll along the valley, and then nothing pushes it back. The first motion is a heavy "particle" (the Higgs mode). The second is a free, massless one (the Goldstone mode).

Part 2 puts a little compass on every point of a grid. Neighbouring compasses like to point the same way. Heat makes them shake. When you cool the grid fast, different regions choose different directions. Where the regions meet, the compasses must turn once around a point. This point is a vortex. It cannot disappear easily, so cooling leaves some vortices behind.

Things to try

  1. Part 1: press "Push up the wall", then "Push along the valley".
    What happens and whyThe first push makes the ball swing. The wall of the valley pushes it back, and the orange curve oscillates with frequency 2μ. The second push makes the ball go round the valley. The valley floor is flat: there is no force along it, so the angle grows in a straight line and the ball never swings back. The numbers show the measured frequency and the formula agree.
  2. Part 1: press Reset (the ball stops in the valley). Set the push size to 1.0 and press "Push to the centre". Then press Reset, set it to 1.5, and push again. Last, push along the valley first and then to the centre with 1.5.
    What happens and whyWith 1.0 the ball climbs the central hump, stops below the top and rolls back: the energy (0.5) is below the hump height (1 for μ² = 1). With 1.5 the energy is 1.125, more than the hump. The ball rolls over the top, through the centre and up the far wall. It then swings through the centre again and again. At this energy the motion does not pick a point of the valley any more: the broken symmetry is restored. The same happens to a real field when you heat it: above a critical temperature the field sits at the centre again. Large pushes up the outer wall also send the ball over the top when it comes back, because there is no friction to take the energy away. The outer wall grows like |φ|⁴, so the ball always turns back: it can never escape. If the ball also moves along the valley, its angular momentum keeps it away from the centre, even with enough energy: it circles round the top of the hump. The readout gives the closest distance.
  3. Part 1: set μ² below 0.
    What happens and whyThe hat turns into a bowl. The valley ring disappears. The ball now swings in every direction with the same frequency √(2|μ²|). Nothing is special about any direction: the symmetry is not broken. The Goldstone motion is gone. This is the answer to "what changes?": there is no free direction any more.
  4. Part 1: add friction and push again.
    What happens and whyFriction slows all motion, so the swing dies out and the ball settles at a point of the valley. It also slows the roll along the valley. That is plain friction, not a force from the hat: the hat itself gives no push along the valley. Without friction (γ = 0), the roll never changes.
  5. Part 2: cool down fast (short time), then slow (long time).
    What happens and whyThe fast cool-down leaves many vortices. The slow one leaves few. When you cool fast, far-apart regions choose their angle without knowing about each other. Where the regions meet in a mismatched way, a vortex gets trapped. When you cool slowly, the regions have time to line up. This is the Kibble–Zurek idea. In one test run, 32 sweeps left about 180 vortices, and 3000 sweeps left about 6. Your numbers will differ, because the process is random.
  6. Part 2: set T = 0.1, then press "Add a long wave". Watch the colours.
    What happens and whyThe wave fades very slowly: it stays for thousands of sweeps. This is the Goldstone mode of the grid. A slow, long twist of the angle costs almost no energy, so nothing pushes it back quickly. Short waves cost more energy and fade fast. The wave is not a vortex: the colours never go once around a point.
  7. Part 2: click on the picture at T = 0.3, then at T = 1.2.
    What happens and whyThe click makes a vortex pair, 12 sites apart. At low T the pair is "bound": the two circles attract, and they may slowly move together and annihilate. At high T the heat pulls pairs apart, and free vortices wander around. The count in the top plot shows this change near T = 0.893.
  8. Part 2: move the temperature slowly from 0.2 to 1.6 and wait about three seconds at each step.
    What happens and whyOrange dots build up a curve. The vortex density is small at low T and grows fast when T passes about 0.9. This is the Kosterlitz–Thouless transition. The curve is smooth, not a sharp jump, because the grid is finite and it takes time to settle.

The physics

Part 1. A complex field φ has two parts, a size and an angle (a phase). The energy depends on the size only. This gives the Mexican hat. The rotation of the angle is a symmetry (called U(1)). When the field sits at a point of the valley, it picks one angle and the symmetry is broken. The motion along the valley has no restoring force. This is the Goldstone mode. In a field with many points, it becomes a massless wave. The rule is Goldstone's theorem: each broken continuous symmetry gives one massless mode.

The motion up the wall is the radial mode. It has a mass: its frequency is ω = 2μ. People call this the "Higgs mode" of a field with a broken global symmetry. Be careful with the word. The real Higgs mechanism needs one more ingredient: a gauge field. With the gauge field, the massless Goldstone mode is "eaten" and the gauge field gets a mass. Nothing like this happens in this room, because there is no gauge field here. The room Vortices That Trap Flux adds it.

Over the hump. The central hump has the height μ⁴/(4λ). A ball with less energy is trapped in the valley and must choose an angle. A ball with more energy rolls through the centre, and its motion is symmetric again. For a field this is symmetry restoration at high energy or high temperature. In the early universe the Higgs field sat at the centre of its hat, and the symmetry was broken when the universe cooled.

Is the theory renormalizable? Yes. The Mexican hat V = −μ²|φ|² + λ|φ|⁴ in four space-time dimensions is renormalizable: the infinities of the quantum theory can be absorbed in μ², λ and the size of the field. That is why the Higgs sector of the Standard Model works. Big pushes do not change this. The real problems are other ones. Quantum corrections pull μ² towards very large values (the hierarchy problem). λ grows with energy, so the φ⁴ theory alone is probably only an effective theory (the "triviality" problem). In the Standard Model, the top quark makes λ smaller at high energy. With the measured masses it becomes slightly negative near 10¹⁰–10¹¹ GeV, so our vacuum is probably metastable, but very long-lived. A term like |φ|⁶ would be non-renormalizable in four dimensions. This classical ball does not show any of these quantum effects.

Part 2. Put the field on a lattice. Keep only the angle at each site. This is the XY model. The computer draws random configurations with the right chances for a given temperature, using the Metropolis rule. This is a Monte Carlo method. A vortex has a winding number: how many times the angle turns around it. In the middle of a vortex the field cannot choose an angle. On the real hat, it would have to climb to the top.

In two dimensions, there is no true long-range order at any T > 0 (the Mermin–Wagner theorem). Long waves destroy it. But there is a sharp change at TKT: below it, vortices and anti-vortices stay in bound pairs, and above it they move freely (KT transition). Kosterlitz and Thouless shared the 2016 Nobel Prize (with Haldane) for this kind of work.

The same maths describes thin films of superfluid helium, and cold atoms. The cool-down test is the Kibble–Zurek mechanism. It was first proposed for the early universe and checked in many laboratory systems.

The math

Show the formulas
V(φ) = −μ²|φ|² + ¼|φ|⁴,   φ = r eiθ The hat. In the code the quartic coefficient is λ = ¼ (the quantity "LAM"). It depends on r only, not on θ. V′(r) = 0   ⇒   r₀² = μ²/(2λ) = 2μ² The valley ring has this radius. Any θ is allowed, so the minimum is a circle. V″(r₀) = −2μ² + 3r₀² = 4μ²   ⇒   ω = 2μ A small swing up the wall is harmonic with this frequency. The mass of the radial mode is 2μ. The angle has no such term: its frequency is 0. V ≈ |μ²||φ|²   ⇒   ω = √(2|μ²|)   (μ² < 0) In the bowl, both directions swing with the same frequency. E = −Σ⟨ij⟩ cos(θi − θj) The XY energy: a sum over neighbouring pairs. It is lowest when all angles are equal. P(accept) = min(1, e−ΔE/T) The Metropolis rule for a trial change of one angle. T is the temperature (units where the coupling and kB are 1). E/N ≈ −2 + T/2   (low T) Every wave mode stores T/2 of energy (equipartition), and a perfect state has −2 per site. The number is shown under the plot. w = (1/2π) Σ□ Δθ,   Δθ ∈ (−π, π] The winding number of one small square: add the four angle steps around it, each wrapped into (−π, π]. It is 0 or ±1. This gives the circles. θ = arg((z − z₊)/(z − z₋)) The imprint of a click: a +1 vortex at z₊ and a −1 vortex at z₋, 12 sites apart. TKT ≈ 0.893 (in units of the coupling) A value from large simulations. The exact rule is TKT = (π/2) ρs, where ρs is the stiffness of the angle field. Bound vortex pairs lower ρs from 1 to about 0.57 at the transition. ΔE(pair, distance R) ≈ 2π J ln(R/a) + 2 Ecore The energy of one vortex–anti-vortex pair grows like the logarithm of the distance. Heat can pay this cost only when the entropy gain is larger. This gives the transition.

Honest limits

Part 1 is one classical ball. It shows the shapes of the motions, but it is not a quantum field. It has no quantum corrections, so it cannot show renormalization, the running of λ or vacuum decay. When the view zooms out for a big push, the heights are squeezed to fit (the caption gives the factor); the positions and the numbers stay exact. Part 2 is the XY model: a classical model with random sampling, not a real material. The "time" of the cool-down is the number of Monte Carlo sweeps, and it is not real time. The exact law for the number of vortices against cool-down time is not drawn here: the plot only shows that slow cooling leaves fewer. The grid has 128 × 128 sites with wrap-around edges, so the transition looks rounded and the density at a given T needs a few seconds to settle. Close to TKT it settles very slowly. The clicked pair is a rough start, not an exact solution.
