Double Slit Flow

One electron wave goes through two slits. Colour shows the phase, brightness shows the probability. The thin lines show how the probability flows. Dots on the screen are single arrivals.

How to use this room
  1. Watch the wave pass the two slits. Wait until dots build up on the screen at the right (about 20 seconds).
  2. Set slits to "top only". The stripes go away. Set it back to "both open". The stripes come back.
  3. Move the slit distance d and the wavelength λ sliders, then press Restart. Compare the three stripe distances below the picture.

What the numbers mean

The wave obeys iħ ∂ψ/∂t = −(ħ²/2m)∇²ψ + Vψ. The code uses the Visscher scheme: the real and the imaginary part of ψ are updated in turn, each from its neighbours on the grid. The total probability stays at 1 (to about 0.1 %) until the wave reaches the soft absorbing border. The walls are hard: ψ = 0 inside them.

The orange curve does not use the simulation. It adds one arrow for every straight path from a slit point to a screen point, each turned by k·r (the action divided by ħ). This is the path sum from the first demo, applied to the slits.

The flow lines follow the probability current v = (ħ/m)·Im(∇ψ/ψ). In the de Broglie–Bohm interpretation a particle really moves on such a line. Standard quantum mechanics gives the same dot statistics without the lines. The lines never cross, so a line that starts in the upper half stays in the upper half.

Check: the far-field stripe distance is about λL/d, where L is the distance from the slits to the screen.

Guide: what you are looking at

In one minute

Send electrons, one at a time, at a wall with two narrow slits. Each electron arrives at one point on the screen, as one small dot. But after thousands of dots, a pattern of stripes appears. Neither slit alone makes these stripes. Richard Feynman said that this experiment contains "the only mystery" of quantum mechanics.

The quantum rule is this. For each way that the electron can go, nature gives a small arrow (an amplitude). Add the arrows of all the ways head to tail. The squared length of the sum is the probability. With two slits there are two ways. At some points on the screen the two arrows point in opposite directions and cancel: a dark stripe. At other points they point the same way: a bright stripe. This is interference.

Things to try

  1. Close one slit (slits → "top only").
    What happens and whyThe stripes go away, and you see one broad spot. With one slit there is only one family of paths, so there is nothing for the arrows to cancel against. The broad spot is the diffraction of a single slit: a narrow slit spreads the wave.
  2. Make the slit distance d larger.
    What happens and whyThe stripes move closer together. The stripe distance is about λL/d. A larger d gives a larger difference in path length for a small step on the screen, so the arrows turn against each other faster.
  3. Make the wavelength λ larger.
    What happens and whyThe stripes move apart. A longer wavelength means the arrow turns more slowly along the path, so you need a larger step on the screen to go from bright to dark. For an electron, λ = h/p: a slower electron has a longer wavelength.
  4. Follow one flow line from the upper slit.
    What happens and whyIt stays in the upper half of the picture. Flow lines cannot cross, and the line in the middle is a line of symmetry. In the de Broglie–Bohm interpretation, this says that an electron that lands in the upper half came through the upper slit. Standard quantum mechanics makes the same predictions for the dots, but it does not say which path the electron took.

The physics

The electron is described by a wave function ψ. It is a complex number at each point: a length and a direction (a phase). The wave moves by the Schrödinger equation. The simulation solves this equation step by step on a grid of 600 × 360 points.

The same result comes from the path integral. Take every path from the source to a screen point. Give it an arrow that turns by its action S divided by ħ. For a free electron, the arrow turns by one full turn for each wavelength of path length. Add all arrows. The room Every path is an arrow shows this sum in detail. Here, the orange curve uses only the straight paths through the slits, and it already gives the stripes.

The dots show that the electron is not a smeared-out wave when you detect it. Each detection gives one point. The wave only gives the probability of each point. This is the Born rule.

The math

Show the formulas
iħ ∂ψ/∂t = −(ħ²/2m) ∇²ψ + V ψ The Schrödinger equation. In the code ħ = m = 1, and V is very large inside the wall. ψ(screen) ≈ a₁ ei k r₁ + a₂ ei k r₂,   k = 2π/λ The two-path approximation: r₁ and r₂ are the distances from the two slits to the screen point. |ψ|² ≈ |a₁|² + |a₂|² + 2|a₁||a₂| cos(k (r₁ − r₂)) The last term is the interference. It is zero with one slit. r₁ − r₂ ≈ d·y / L   ⇒   stripe distance Δy ≈ λ L / d Valid when the screen is far away (L much larger than d). v = (ħ/m) Im(∇ψ / ψ) The velocity of the flow lines. It is the probability current divided by the probability density.

Honest limits

The world here is two-dimensional, and the wall is perfectly hard. The source is one wave packet, not a stream of separate electrons. The dots are drawn at random from |ψ|² at the screen; a real detector would do the same. The screen is not very far away, so λL/d is only approximate. The three numbers below the picture differ by a few percent for this reason.
