Tunnelling in Imaginary Time

Quantum amplitudes are turning arrows. Turn time by 90° in the complex plane, and the arrows stop turning: they become plain positive weights. Then a computer can draw paths like a random walk, and you can watch a particle tunnel as a kink in the path.

How to use this room
  1. In part 1, drag the rotation angle θ slider from 0° to 90°, or press "Rotate time". Watch the coloured arrows stop turning and become plain positive numbers.
  2. In part 2, watch the white line. It sits in one valley and sometimes jumps to the other one. Each jump is a "kink". Wait until the number "from the Monte-Carlo correlator" appears (it needs 1500 samples).
  3. Move the barrier steepness λ and Planck constant ħ sliders. Watch how the jumps get rarer or more frequent, and how the splitting ΔE changes.

What the numbers mean

With real time t, each path has weight eiS/ħ, a unit arrow. Its angle is the action. Paths with a big bump have a big action, so their arrows spin fast and cancel. This is interference.

Put t = τe−iθ. Then iS/ħ = (q)(−sinθ + i cosθ), with q = m(Δ²+π²c²/2)/(2ħτ). The arrow length is e−q sinθ. The turning rate is q cosθ. At θ = 90° the turning stops. The weight is e−SE/ħ, a positive number. A path far from the straight line is suppressed. Now the sum is a statistics problem.

The free-particle propagator is K = √(m/(2πiħt)) · exp(imΔ²/(2ħt)). At θ = 90° it becomes √(m/(2πħτ)) · e−mΔ²/(2ħτ). This is the heat kernel: diffusion of a random walker. The row "K" below the plots shows this exact formula.

The Gaussian integral over c has a closed form, √(π/A), with A = (mπ²/4ħτ)(sinθ − i cosθ). At θ = 0 the integral converges only because of the oscillation (a Fresnel integral). The finite sum (51 paths) cuts the spiral, so it differs a little there.

What the numbers mean

The particle has mass 1 in the potential V(x) = λ(x²−a²)². It has two wells, at x = ±a. In imaginary time the weight of a path is e−SE/ħ, with SE = ∫[ẋ²/2 + V(x)]dτ. Now the potential is turned upside down in the equation of motion: ẍ = V′(x). A solution that rolls from −a to +a is the kink.

First integral: ẋ²/2 = V. So ẋ = √(2λ)(a²−x²). The solution is x = a·tanh(ωτ/2) with ω = 2a√(2λ) = √(8λa²). This ω is also the oscillation frequency at the bottom of one well, since V″(a) = 8λa².

The instanton action is S₀ = ∫−aa √(2V) dx = √(2λ)·(4a³/3) = (4√2/3)·√λ·a³. One kink costs the factor e−S₀/ħ. The page shows S₀ from a numerical integral and from this formula.

The two lowest levels are a symmetric and an antisymmetric state. Their gap ΔE = E₁ − E₀ is the tunnelling splitting. A particle that starts in the left well oscillates between the wells with period 2πħ/ΔE. In imaginary time, the correlator ⟨x(0)x(τ)⟩ falls like e−ΔEτ/ħ, since every kink flips the sign of x.

Semiclassical (instanton gas) estimate: ΔE ≈ 2ħω·√(6S₀/(πħ))·e−S₀/ħ. This is approximate: it ignores corrections of order ħ/S₀. For a dilute gas of kinks, the number of kinks on a path of length β is about βΔE/(2ħ).

Errors you can see: the path has N slices, so the step is ε = β/N. A big ε adds a small error to ΔE. The Monte-Carlo fit also has noise. For a very high barrier, jumps are so rare that the sampler needs a long time. That is the same physics: tunnelling is slow.

Guide: what you are looking at

In one minute

A quantum particle can be in one of two valleys, with a hill between them. Classically, if it has too little energy, it stays in its valley for ever. In quantum mechanics it can end up in the other valley anyway. This is tunnelling.

How can you compute it? The quantum rule gives every path a turning arrow, and you must add all arrows. The arrows spin, so they mostly cancel, and the sum is hard to do on a computer. There is a mathematical trick: let time run along a different direction in the complex plane, so that "time" becomes "imaginary". This is a Wick rotation. After it, the arrows do not turn. Each path gets a plain positive weight, and a wild path gets a small weight. Now a computer can pick paths at random, in the same way as a random walk. Part 1 shows this change. Part 2 uses it.

In part 2, you watch such random paths in a landscape with two valleys. A path mostly sits in one valley. Sometimes it jumps quickly to the other valley. Each jump is an instanton. How often the jumps happen tells you how fast the real particle tunnels. Imaginary time is a tool for the calculation. No particle really moves in imaginary time.

Things to try

Part 1

  1. At θ = 0, do the arrows add up to a big arrow or a small one?
    What happens and whyA small one. There are 51 arrows, each of length 1, and in a perfect case they would add to about 5. The sum is about 1.3 only, so most of the arrows cancel. The wild paths (large bump c) have a large action, and their arrows spin fast and point in all directions. The paths close to the straight line have a nearly stationary phase, so they agree with each other and survive. This is stationary phase.
  2. Slide θ to 90°. Which paths dominate now? Does the sum turn or stay on the positive axis?
    What happens and whyThe straight and nearly straight paths dominate. The wild paths shrink to nothing, because their weight is e−q sin θ and q is large for a big bump. At 90° each weight is the positive number e−SE/ħ. Nothing turns, so the white sum arrow lies on the positive axis (direction 0°) and equals the exact cyan dot. The middle picture shrinks to a bunch of short arrows along the axis.
  3. Stop at θ = 45°.
    What happens and whyYou see both effects: the arrows turn a bit and they shrink a bit. This is the path between the two worlds. The room works with a complex time t = τe−iθ, and every angle between 0 and 90° is a valid way to compute the same exact answer (the propagator).
  4. At θ = 90° compare the propagator K to the "heat kernel".
    What happens and whyThey are the same number. The quantum propagator of a free particle in imaginary time is the diffusion kernel of a random walker. This is the reason a computer can sample paths like a random walk.

Part 2

  1. Raise λ (a higher, steeper barrier). Do the jumps get rarer? By how much does ΔE fall?
    What happens and whyYes, the jumps get rarer. A kink costs the factor e−S₀/ħ, and S₀ grows like √λ. With the default ħ = 1, S₀/ħ is only about 3 to 5, so the fall is moderate: ΔE goes from about 0.57 at λ = 3 to about 0.30 at λ = 6. Set ħ to 0.5 and try the same: ΔE falls from about 0.021 to about 0.0025, a factor of 8. The fall is exponential, and it is dramatic when S₀/ħ is large.
  2. Make ħ larger. Does the particle tunnel more or less?
    What happens and whyMore. ħ sets how "quantum" the world is. A larger ħ makes e−S₀/ħ larger, so kinks are cheaper. At λ = 3 the splitting ΔE goes from about 0.021 at ħ = 0.5 to about 0.57 at ħ = 1 and about 1.7 at ħ = 1.5. In the limit ħ → 0 the particle never tunnels. This is the classical limit.
  3. Make the path length β longer.
    What happens and whyMore jumps fit on the path. The average number of kinks is about βΔE/(2ħ), the number you see in the "dilute-gas guess". The correlator also covers a longer τ range, so the fitted ΔE is more precise.
  4. Watch the cyan curve and the white line at a jump.
    What happens and whyThe jump has the shape of the classical kink (a hyperbolic tangent), with some noise from the random sampling. The jump takes a time of order 1/ω. In between the jumps, the path stays in a valley for a long time. This is the "gas" of well-separated kinks.
  5. Compare the amber line to the teal dashed line, and the big numbers.
    What happens and whyAfter 1500 samples they usually agree within about 15 % (the number turns green). The Monte-Carlo method knows nothing about the Schrödinger equation. It only samples paths with the weight e−SE/ħ. Yet the decay of the correlator gives the same ΔE as the exact solution. This is the path integral at work.
  6. Compare the instanton estimate with the exact ΔE.
    What happens and whyAt the default values (λ = 3, ħ = 1) the estimate is about 0.93 and the exact value is about 0.57, so the estimate is too high by about 60 %. At ħ = 0.5, λ = 6 (S₀/ħ above 9) the estimate is about 0.0028 and the exact value is about 0.0025: only 13 % off. The formula is a large-S₀/ħ result. It gets better as S₀/ħ grows.

The physics

The path integral says that the amplitude to go from A to B is the sum over all paths of eiS/ħ, where S is the action. This sum is the propagator. The room Every path is an arrow shows this in detail. For a free particle it can be done exactly, and part 1 uses this to test the fan of paths.

A Wick rotation sets time t = τe−iθ and turns θ from 0 to 90°. At 90° time is imaginary time. The weight changes from eiS/ħ to e−SE/ħ, where SE is the "Euclidean action". It is the energy-like sum ∫(½ẋ² + V) dτ with a plus sign for the potential. A path with a large SE is suppressed. The sum is now like a statistical average, and you can sample it with Monte Carlo: propose a change of the path, accept it with a probability that depends on how the weight changes (the Metropolis rule), and repeat.

A second use of imaginary time: for long τ, the weight is dominated by the lowest energy levels, because a state with energy E decays like e−Eτ/ħ. So the correlator ⟨x(0)x(τ)⟩ falls like e−(E₁−E₀)τ/ħ. The gap E₁ − E₀ between the two lowest levels is the tunnelling splitting ΔE. In a symmetric double well, the lowest state E₀ is "particle in both valleys" and the next one E₁ is the same with a sign change. A particle that starts in the left valley then oscillates between the valleys with a period of 2πħ/ΔE. So a small ΔE means slow tunnelling.

Which paths matter? Each valley has a ground state with energy about ħω/2. A path that stays in one valley has a small action. A path with one jump has the extra action S₀ = ∫√(2V) dx between the valleys. This is the instanton action. A "dilute gas" of such kinks, far apart, explains ΔE ∝ e−S₀/ħ. This idea is the starting point of many calculations in quantum field theory. In gauge theories instantons are the analogue in four dimensions (Coleman's lectures give a classic introduction).

What is exact in this room: the energy levels (diagonalised on a grid), S₀, the kink shape, the free propagator and the Gaussian integral in part 1. What is simulation: the live path and the correlator. What is interpretation: the picture of a gas of kinks and the formula for ΔE built on it. What is cartoon: the hand-placed cyan kink, and the one-parameter family of paths in part 1.

Honest limits

Imaginary time is a mathematical tool. It gives exact energy levels and decay rates of the ground-state sector, but a path in imaginary time is not a movie of what the real particle does. The instanton formula is approximate, and at the default settings it is off by tens of percent (see the first big numbers). The fan in part 1 uses only a one-parameter family of paths, so its sum has the right shape but not the right size. The finite sum of 51 paths at θ = 0 differs from the exact Fresnel value. The Monte-Carlo sampler uses 300 time slices, so ΔE has a small step error (ε = β/300), and it has statistical noise. For a very high barrier or a small ħ, jumps are rare, and the sampler needs a long time: that is the same physics as slow tunnelling. The kink-pair and flip moves are there to help the sampler cross the barrier, and they keep the exact weight.
