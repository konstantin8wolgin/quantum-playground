Light Takes Every Path

A photon does not choose one way. It tries every way. Each way gives a small arrow, and the arrows are added. Three examples: a mirror, a glass surface that bends light, and a thin sheet of glass.

How to use this room
  1. Part 1: look at the spiral of arrows. Only the arrows in the middle add up. Then move the detector slider to the middle (x = 0) and press Scrape the mirror. Light now goes to a place it did not go before.
  2. Part 2: move the glass index n slider. The white path is the fastest path, and it obeys Snell's law. Read the two numbers in the green box.
  3. Part 3: make the sheet thicker with the thickness slider. The reflection goes up and down between 0 and about 15 %.

1. The mirror: every strip is a path

A lamp S sends light to a detector P. The light may touch the mirror at any point. We cut the mirror in strips. Each strip gives one path S → strip → P, and one arrow. The arrow turns by one full turn for each wavelength of path length.

What the numbers mean

The path through strip i has length Li. Its arrow has angle k Li with k = 2π/λ. Each arrow is the sum of the tiny arrows inside its strip, so its length is the strip width times sinc(k L' w / 2). A long arrow means that the path length hardly changes along the strip. Soft ends fade the arrows near the mirror ends to zero. A hard edge adds small ripples to the sum.

For an endless mirror the sum is exactly known in the limit of short waves. Near the equal-angle point L ≈ L₀ + ½ L'' (x − x*)², so the sum is a Fresnel integral. Its size squared is 2π/(k L''), where L'' = h₁²/r₁³ + h₂²/r₂³. The number in the green box is the strip sum divided by this value. With 60 strips it is within about 1 % of 1.

"Scrape" keeps only the strips whose arrow points into the half plane of the total sum, as seen from the detector you chose. The kept strips form a pattern with period d. Light is then sent to the detector with d (sin θout − sin θin) = m λ, with m = 1. The line "grating check" measures d from the pattern near the middle and shows m. It is close to 1, but not exact, because the strips are coarse and the lamp is near.

2. Why light bends: the fastest path

Light goes from S in air to P in glass. It may cross the surface at any point. The arrow of a path turns by 2π (Lair + n Lglass)/λ. Far from the fastest path, the arrows turn quickly and cancel. Near the fastest path, they turn slowly and add.

What the numbers mean

The code finds the point of least travel time by a search on the time curve only. It does not use Snell's law. Then it measures the two angles from the vertical. The ratio of their sines is n. This is Snell's law, found as the place where the path integral has no turning arrows. Glass is "slower" because the arrow turns n times faster inside it.

Fermat's principle (light takes the path of least time) is a result of the path integral: only paths near the least-time path have arrows that add.

3. A sheet of glass: two arrows

Light hits a glass sheet in air (n = 1.5). A little light is reflected at the front surface. A little light is reflected at the back surface. Each gives one arrow of length 0.2 (so 4 % of the light, 0.2² = 0.04). The front arrow points backward (reversed). The back arrow is turned by 2π · 2nt/λ, where t is the thickness.

What the numbers mean

At normal incidence, the amplitude reflected by the front surface is −r with r = (n−1)/(n+1). Inside the glass, the back surface reflects +r. Feynman uses two arrows: front −r, back r eiδ, with δ = 4π n t/λ. The sum has the size R = 4r² sin²(δ/2). Its largest value is 4r² = 16 % for n = 1.5.

The exact answer also includes light that bounces back and forth inside the sheet. This is a series of smaller arrows: −r, then (1−r²) r eiδ, then (1−r²) r³ e2iδ, and so on. The series gives R = 4r² sin²(δ/2) / [(1−r²)² + 4r² sin²(δ/2)]. The largest value is 4r²/(1+r²)² = 14.79 %. The two-arrow value is a good first answer, but it is not exact: it is too large by 8 % at the top.

Guide: what you are looking at

In one minute

Richard Feynman's idea is simple. When a photon goes from a lamp to a detector, it does not go on one path. It goes on every path. Each path gives a small arrow (an amplitude). The arrow turns as the photon travels: one full turn for each wavelength of path. Add all the arrows head to tail. The squared length of the sum is the probability that the photon arrives.

These arrows are the path integral for light. Paths with a very different length have arrows that point in all directions, so they cancel. Only paths near the shortest (or longest) one have arrows that point the same way. This is why a mirror seems to work with one path (angle in = angle out) and why glass seems to bend light with one path (Snell's law). In both cases, one path is only the place where the arrows add up. See the path-integral room and the double-slit room for the same rule with electrons.

Things to try

  1. Part 1: look at the spiral. Which strips make the sum?
    What happens and whyThe strips in the middle, near the equal-angle point. There the path length changes slowly, so neighbouring arrows point the same way and form a straight chain. At the ends the chain curls into circles that add up to almost nothing. This is the stationary-phase idea: only paths where the length is at its minimum count. The check number in the green box says the strip sum has the size that the stationary-phase formula predicts.
  2. Part 1: make λ smaller.
    What happens and whyThe spiral at the ends becomes tighter and the straight part in the middle becomes shorter. The part of the mirror that matters (the first Fresnel zone) has a width of about √(λ/L''), so it shrinks like √λ. For very short waves, light seems to touch the mirror at one point only. This is the classical limit: rays.
  3. Part 1: put the detector at x = 0 and press Scrape the mirror.
    What happens and whyThe mirror keeps only the strips whose arrows point with the sum for this detector. All kept arrows now add. The light at x = 0 grows by a large factor, although light on a normal mirror would not go there from this lamp. The kept strips form a pattern, like the grooves of a reflection grating. The light goes where the grating law says: d (sin θout − sin θin) = λ. Move the detector away and the bright spot stays at x = 0 in the graph on the right. Change λ, and the bright spot moves, because a grating bends each colour to another angle.
  4. Part 2: move n from 1.0 to 2.0.
    What happens and whyAt n = 1.0 the white path is a straight line. As n grows, the white path crosses the surface closer to the point above the detector, and the light bends toward the vertical inside the glass. In glass, each path is longer in wave count (n times), so it is "slower". The path of least time is then one that stays shorter inside the glass. The ratio sin θ₁ / sin θ₂ stays equal to n.
  5. Part 3: set the thickness to 0 and then increase it.
    What happens and whyAt t = 0 the two arrows have equal length and point in opposite directions. They cancel: no reflection. At 2nt = λ/2 (about 92 nm for green light) the back arrow has turned half a turn, and now both arrows point the same way: R is at the top, 16 % with two arrows and 14.8 % exact. At 2nt = λ the arrows cancel again. The pattern repeats every λ/(2n) of thickness, about 183 nm. This is the reason for the colours of soap bubbles and oil films.
  6. Part 3: change λ.
    What happens and whyThe maximum and minimum positions move: they depend on the ratio t/λ. A thick sheet reflects different colours differently. For a very thick sheet (more than a few thousand wavelengths of white light), the maxima for the different colours overlap and the colour goes away.

The physics

This room follows the spirit of Feynman's popular book QED: The Strange Theory of Light and Matter (1985). The pictures here are drawn new.

The rule has three steps. (1) For each way the photon can go, draw an arrow. Its length depends on how likely the process is. Its direction is the phase, which is the action divided by ħ. For light on a straight path, the phase is 2π times the path length divided by the wavelength. (2) Add the arrows. This is superposition. (3) Square the length of the sum.

The mirror shows stationary phase. When the path length is at a minimum, the phase does not change to first order, so arrows add. The strips near the minimum have a width of about one Fresnel zone. The sum is then a Fresnel integral and the length squared is 2π/(k L''). When you scrape away the strips that point backward, you build a grating by hand: light now comes to a place where the stationary-phase point of the full mirror would not send it.

Refraction is the same sum, but the phase turns n times faster in glass. The place where the phase is stationary gives Snell's law. The sheet of glass shows that even a "partial" reflection is a sum of two paths. Each surface does not "decide" to reflect 4 %. The 4 % is the length of one arrow. The thickness changes the relative direction of the two arrows, and so R changes between 0 and about 15 %.

A Feynman diagram is a bookkeeping of such arrows. Each line and each corner of a diagram stands for one factor of an arrow, and the whole diagram is one arrow in the sum. The room "Feynman diagrams from a path integral" shows how this works for the photon and the electron. This room gives the simplest case: only the photon path, with the glass and the mirror as given. In the full theory (QED) the glass atoms also take part, and the number r = (n − 1)/(n + 1) comes from a sum over many Feynman diagrams with the photon and the electrons in the glass.

Honest limits

Light is treated as a scalar wave: there is no polarisation and no change of r with angle (in parts 1 and 2). The arrows have a fixed length per strip. Real light from a point lamp gets weaker with distance, and this is left out. The path sum for the mirror uses a flat mirror that reflects every strip equally, with no atoms. Part 3 is at normal incidence only; the thin arrows come from the standard Fresnel amplitudes, which are not derived here. The "two arrows" picture is Feynman's approximation and is 8 % too high at the maximum. The scrape is a one-detector design: the strips are kept for one detector and one wavelength. Because the mirror has only 40–80 strips, the measured grating law is close to 1 but not exact. The wave sums here do not show that a photon "really" takes all paths. They are a way to compute the correct probability.
