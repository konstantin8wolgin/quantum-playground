Path Integral Playground

A particle goes from A to B. Quantum mechanics adds up every path. Two views: real time (arrows that cancel) and imaginary time (a random walk you can sample).

1 · Real time: every path is an arrow

Each path gets an arrow of length 1 that turns by the angle S/ħ, where S is the action of the path. The amplitude is the sum of all arrows. A particle feels a constant force F (for example a charge in an electric field). It starts at A and arrives at B after time T = 1.

Paths, colored by phase relative to the classical path (dashed). The band shows paths whose phase is within π of the classical phase.
Arrows added head to tail, in order of path height. The bold line is the total.
Try:
  • Move ħ to the left. The band gets thin and only the classical parabola survives. This is how classical physics comes out of quantum physics.
  • Move the highlight slider to the end. The far paths sit in the tight curls of the spiral: they cancel.
  • Set the force to 0. The surviving paths move to a straight line.

2 · Imaginary time: the path becomes a random walk

Replace time t by −iτ. The turning arrow eiS/ħ becomes a positive weight e−SE/ħ. Now it is a probability, and a computer can draw random paths with this weight (Metropolis algorithm). The path is a closed loop of length β = 1/temperature.

The current sampled path x(τ). Faint lines are earlier samples.
Bars: where the path spends time. Line: exact answer |ψ|² (transfer matrix). Gray: the potential V(x).
Try:
  • Harmonic: watch the bars converge to the line. The random walk gives the exact ground state.
  • Double well with ħ ≈ 0.5: the path stays in one well for a long time, then jumps. These jumps are instantons: quantum tunneling.
  • Double well with ħ = 0.2: the line shows two peaks, but the bars show one. The sampler is stuck, because a jump is too rare. This is a real problem of the method.
  • Make β small (hot). The loop gets short and the quantum spread turns into thermal spread.
