Path Integral Playground

A particle goes from A to B. Quantum mechanics adds up every path. Two views: real time (arrows that cancel) and imaginary time (a random walk you can sample).

How to use this room
  1. In part 1, move the ħ slider to the left. The pale band of surviving paths gets thin around the dashed line. Move it back to the right and the band grows.
  2. Move the highlight one path slider. The big dot walks along the spiral on the right. Paths in the middle make the spiral grow. Paths far away curl up and cancel.
  3. In part 2, choose the double well and wait. The bold loop jumps between two valleys. The blue bars slowly grow towards the black curve.

1 · Real time: every path is an arrow

Each path gets an arrow of length 1 that turns by the angle S/ħ, where S is the action of the path. The amplitude is the sum of all arrows. A particle feels a constant force F (for example a charge in an electric field). It starts at A and arrives at B after time T = 1.

Paths, colored by phase relative to the classical path (dashed). The band shows the paths whose arrow points within half a turn (π) of the best arch. The best arch is close to, but not exactly on, the dashed classical path, because all paths here are arches of one shape.
Arrows added head to tail, in order of path height. The bold line is the total.
Try:
  • Move ħ to the left. The band gets thin and only the classical parabola survives. This is how classical physics comes out of quantum physics.
  • Move the highlight slider to the end. The far paths sit in the tight curls of the spiral: they cancel.
  • Set the force to 0. The surviving paths move to a straight line.

2 · Imaginary time: the path becomes a random walk

Replace time t by −iτ. The turning arrow eiS/ħ becomes a positive weight e−SE/ħ. Now it is a probability, and a computer can draw random paths with this weight (Metropolis algorithm). The path is a closed loop of length β = 1/temperature.

The current sampled path x(τ). Faint lines are earlier samples.
Bars: where the path spends time. Line: exact answer (transfer matrix): the probability density for this β. For large β it is |ψ|² of the ground state. Gray: the potential V(x).
Try:
  • Harmonic: watch the bars converge to the line. The random walk gives the exact ground state.
  • Double well with ħ ≈ 0.5: the path stays in one well for a long time, then jumps. These jumps are instantons: quantum tunneling.
  • Double well with ħ = 0.2: the line shows two peaks, but the bars show one. The sampler is stuck, because a jump is too rare. This is a real problem of the method.
  • Make β small (hot). The loop gets short and the quantum spread turns into thermal spread.

Guide: what you are looking at

In one minute

A ball goes from A to B. In everyday life it takes one path. In quantum mechanics, a particle takes every path. Each path gets a small arrow (an amplitude). The arrow turns by an angle that depends on the path. You add all the arrows head to tail. The squared length of the total gives the probability.

Almost all paths cancel each other, because their arrows point in all directions. Only the paths close to the classical one add up. This is why a big object seems to follow one path. Part 1 of this room lets you see this cancellation, and shows what happens when you make the world "less quantum".

Part 2 uses a trick: it runs time in an imaginary direction. The turning arrows become plain positive weights. Now a computer can pick random paths with the right weights, and count. This finds the lowest energy state and also shows quantum tunnelling.

Things to try

  1. Part 1: move ħ to the left.
    What happens and whyThe pale band gets thin and only paths very close to the dashed line survive. The arrow turns by S/ħ. A smaller ħ means that a small change of the path gives a large change of phase, so neighbouring paths cancel quickly. The band width goes like √ħ. In the limit of small ħ, only the classical path is left. This is how classical physics comes out of quantum physics (the classical limit).
  2. Part 1: move the highlight slider to the end.
    What happens and whyThe dot sits in a tight curl of the spiral. The phase changes with the square of the height a. Far from the best arch the arrows turn faster and faster, so they go round in small circles and add almost nothing. Near the middle the arrows turn slowly and add up in a nearly straight line.
  3. Part 1: set the force to 0.
    What happens and whyThe surviving paths move to a straight line (the best arch has height 0). With no force, the classical particle moves in a straight line at constant speed.
  4. Part 2, harmonic spring: watch the bars.
    What happens and whyThe bars converge to the black curve. The random paths, with weights e−S/ħ, give the exact ground state. At the start the bars are noisy, because few samples exist. The two numbers ⟨x²⟩ also come together.
  5. Part 2, double well, ħ about 0.5.
    What happens and whyThe path stays in one valley for a long time. Then it jumps to the other valley. Each jump is an instanton. This is the picture of tunnelling in imaginary time. The counter shows the jumps. A jump costs an action of about 1.89 in these units. So it has a weight of about e−1.89/ħ, which is 0.02 for ħ = 0.5.
  6. Part 2, double well, ħ = 0.2.
    What happens and whyThe black curve has two peaks, but the bars show only one. The sampler is stuck in one valley. The jump weight is now e−1.89/0.2, about 10−4, so the computer almost never sees a jump. This is a real problem of the Monte Carlo method. It is not a property of nature. A longer run, or a smarter method, would fill the second peak.
  7. Part 2: make β small (hot).
    What happens and whyThe loop gets short. The path has little time to move, so it stays close to one point. The quantum spread turns into the ordinary thermal spread: for the spring, ⟨x²⟩ goes to 1/β, the classical value.

The physics

The usual way to describe a particle is the wave function and the Schrödinger equation. Feynman found an equivalent way: the path integral. The amplitude to go from A to B is the sum over all paths of an arrow that turns by action S divided by ħ. The action is the integral of (kinetic energy − potential energy) along the path. The room Double slit flow uses the same sum for just two families of paths.

Why do big things follow one path? For a big object, S is much larger than ħ, so the phase S/ħ changes very fast from path to path. Only near a path where S does not change at first order, the arrows stay together. This is called stationary phase. The paths where S is stationary obey Newton's law. So the classical path is the winner of the sum. Part 1 shows this with the band: it is the stationary-phase region.

Part 2 uses imaginary time. You replace the time t by −iτ (a Wick rotation). Then eiS/ħ becomes e−SE/ħ, a positive number. Paths with a low "Euclidean action" SE are more likely. A computer can now draw random paths with these weights. This is a Monte Carlo method (here: the Metropolis algorithm). A long loop selects the ground state. A short loop gives a hot system. The same method, on a lattice, is how physicists compute with quarks and gluons. The room Tunnelling in imaginary time goes further with the double well.

Honest limits

Part 1 does not sum over all paths. It sums over one family of smooth arches, and (with roughness) a few more wiggles. The real path integral has infinitely many jagged paths. Part 1 also leaves out the normalisation constant. So the picture shows how the cancellation works, not a complete calculation. Part 2 is real Monte Carlo, but the loop has only 128 steps, the samples are correlated, and imaginary time is not real time: the jumps happen in τ, and do not tell you how long tunnelling takes in real time. The black "exact" curve is a numerical matrix calculation. For small β it is the thermal density, not the ground state.
