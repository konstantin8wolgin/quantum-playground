A Phase Without a Force

A thin magnet hides behind the wall, between the two slits. Its magnetic field stays inside it. The electron wave never touches the field. Yet the stripes on the screen move when you change the field. The field does not push the electron. It turns the arrow of each path.

How to use this room
  1. Watch the wave pass the two slits. The blue bars on the right build up over time and settle on the orange curve. The run ends at time 760.
  2. Move the flux slider (it restarts the run). Compare the orange curve with the grey dashed curve, which is the pattern without flux. Try 0.5 and 1.0.
  3. Tick move the cut line during a run. The colours change, but the stripes do not. Set view to "probability only" to see the colours go away.

What the numbers mean

Units: ħ = m = e = 1. The wave obeys i ∂ψ/∂t = ½(−i∇ − A)²ψ + Vψ. Outside the thin magnet the field is zero: B = ∇×A = 0. But A is not zero. Its line integral around the magnet is the flux: ∮A·dl = Φ. A wave that goes around the magnet gets the phase α = 2πΦ/Φ₀. Here Φ₀ is one flux quantum (h/e).

simulation The grid hops carry the phase (the Peierls rule). Choose a cut line from the magnet to the border. Every hop that crosses the cut gets the factor e±iα. This includes the hops of length 2, because the 4th-order Laplacian uses them. The Hamiltonian is now complex, so the real-and-imaginary scheme of the double-slit room does not work. This room uses the leapfrog step ψ(t+Δt) = ψ(t−Δt) − 2iΔt·Hψ(t), with Δt = 0.15. The total probability stays within 1 % until the wave reaches the absorbing border.

exact The orange curve adds one arrow for every straight path from a slit point to a screen point. The arrow turns by k·r. Paths through the top slit turn by α more. The stripes move by −Φ/Φ₀ of one stripe distance (the sign shows the direction). The shift repeats with period 1. So Φ/Φ₀ = 0.5 puts a dark stripe in the middle, and Φ/Φ₀ = 1 gives the pattern of Φ = 0.

Gauge. The cut line has no physical meaning. Move it, and the wave changes by the factor eiα in the region between the old and the new cut. The colours change. The probability, and so the screen pattern, does not. This is a U(1) gauge change. The check "other cut line" compares two runs: the relative difference is about 10−12, which is rounding.

Story. Nature adds up phases. Every path has an arrow. A magnetic potential gives each path one extra turn of its arrow. The size of the turn depends only on how the path winds around the flux. The group of such turns is U(1), the circle. No force acts on the electron: the electron never feels a push, but the sum of its arrows changes. The effect was predicted by Ehrenberg and Siday (1949) and by Aharonov and Bohm (1959). Tonomura's group saw it in 1986.

Limits. The magnet here is a hard disc of radius 4 cells with ψ = 0. It scatters a little wave, so the simulated stripes are not exactly the orange curve. The shift is still the same to about ±0.05 stripe distance. The screen is not in the far field, so stripe distances change slowly with height.

Guide: what you are looking at

In one minute

This is the double-slit experiment with one change. A very thin magnet sits behind the wall, between the two slits. Its magnetic field is only inside the magnet. Everywhere the electron can go, the field is zero. So no magnetic force acts on the electron.

Yet the stripes on the screen move when you change the strength of the magnet. How? The magnet changes the phase of the electron wave. A wave that goes above the magnet gets a different phase from a wave that goes below it. When the two waves meet at the screen, the stripes show the difference. This is the Aharonov–Bohm effect. It tells us that the vector potential, and not only the field, matters in quantum physics.

Things to try

  1. Set flux to 0.5. Where does the middle stripe go?
    What happens and whyThe middle stripe, which is bright at zero flux, becomes dark. The whole pattern moves by half a stripe distance. A flux of 0.5 Φ₀ gives α = 180°. The arrows from the two slits, which pointed the same way in the middle, now point in opposite directions and cancel.
  2. Set flux to 1.0. Do the stripes come back?
    What happens and whyYes. The pattern is the same as for zero flux. One flux unit gives α = 360°, one full turn. A full turn of an arrow changes nothing. So the shift repeats with period 1: Φ = 0, 1, 2 give the same stripes. You can check the numbers below: the shift of the simulation, the path sum, and −Φ/Φ₀ (mod 1) agree. The simulation can differ by a few hundredths of a stripe distance.
  3. Tick "move the cut line" during a run. Do the stripes on the screen change?
    What happens and whyThe colours change in the region between the old and the new cut line. The stripes do not change. Moving the cut line is a change of gauge: it multiplies ψ by a phase factor that depends on the place. Probabilities do not see such a factor. To check this with numbers, finish one run with the cut line at "right". Change the checkbox, press Restart, and finish a second run with the same flux. The relative difference of the two screen patterns is then shown: about 10−12, which is rounding error.
  4. Try flux 0.25, then 0.75.
    What happens and whyThe pattern moves by a quarter of a stripe distance, and then by three quarters. At 0.75 it looks like a shift by a quarter in the other direction (−0.25), because a shift by a whole stripe distance gives the same picture.
  5. Change the wavelength λ.
    What happens and whyThe stripe distance changes (it is about λ·L/d). The shift, in units of the stripe distance, stays the same. The phase α depends on the flux only, and not on the wavelength.

The physics

Classically, a charge feels the force from the electric and magnetic fields. In quantum mechanics, a wave ψ carries a phase, and the vector potential A changes this phase along a path. The change of the phase along a path is the integral of A along it. For two paths that go on two sides of the magnet, the difference is the integral of A around the closed loop. That integral equals the magnetic flux Φ inside the loop. This holds even where the field B is zero along the paths.

So each path gets one extra turn of its arrow, and this turn depends only on how the path winds around the flux. In the path integral language, you add a phase to each path. The phase factors form the group U(1): the circle. The flux that gives a full turn is one flux quantum, Φ₀ = h/e for an electron.

The cut line shows the idea of gauge symmetry. You can choose A in many ways, and you can move the cut line, and the physical result stays the same. Only the phase around a closed loop is real. This loop phase is a Wilson loop. The room Phases Around a Loop uses it on a lattice, and the room Vortices That Trap Flux shows flux that is trapped in whole packets. The room Double slit flow is this room without the magnet.

Honest limits

The simulation is two-dimensional. The magnet is a hard disc of radius 4 cells that the wave cannot enter. This disc scatters some wave, so the simulated stripes are not exactly the orange curve. The shift agrees to about ±0.05 stripe distance. The screen is not in the far field, so the stripe distance changes slowly with height. The orange path sum uses straight paths only. In a real experiment (Tonomura's group used an electron microscope and a tiny ring-shaped magnet) the shielding of the field is never perfect; the 1986 experiments took great care to show that it was. People still argue about how to explain the effect in words (is the potential "real"?). The calculation itself is not in doubt.
