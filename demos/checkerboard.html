Feynman's Checkerboard

A particle with mass, built from the simplest paths there are. It moves one step left or right in every tick of the clock, always at light speed. Each time it turns around, the arrow of the path shrinks and turns by a quarter turn. Add all the arrows, and you get the Dirac wave of an electron in one space dimension.

How to use this room
  1. Part 1: press new path. Count the turns R of the path. Look at its arrow and at the chain of arrows below. The chain adds all paths to one end point. The sum is checked against a count of all paths.
  2. Part 2: set the mass m to 0. Only the two edges of the light cone are lit. Then raise m and watch the inside of the cone fill with waves.
  3. Part 3: make the grid finer (more steps N). The checkerboard sum comes closer to the exact wave. Read the order of convergence. Part 4: a packet on the checkerboard moves slower than light.
New to this? Read the guide at the bottom of the page. Unknown words are explained in the glossary.

Part 1 · Zig-zag paths and their arrows

Time goes down. In each tick the particle jumps one cell left or right. A path that turns R times gets the arrow (−iεm)R. That means: length (εm)R, and a quarter turn clockwise for each reversal. The wave at an end point is the sum of the arrows of all paths that end there.

What the numbers mean

A path with N steps has N−1 places where it can turn. A path with R turns chooses R of these places, so there are C(N−1, R) turn patterns and 2 first directions. For one end point the number N(R) is a product of two binomials: the runs to the right and the runs to the left are split into parts. The test compares this formula with a list of all paths.

The recursion goes one tick at a time: ψR(x, t+ε) = ψR(x−ε, t) − iεm ψL(x−ε, t), and the mirror rule for ψL. Here ψR is the part that arrived with a step to the right. The first step costs nothing. The recursion adds up exactly the same arrows as the brute-force sum, so the difference is rounding only (about 1e-16).

Part 2 · The light cone

Sum the arrows for every point of the space-time triangle. Time goes down, space goes across. A particle without mass never turns. It has one path only, and that path runs along the edge of the light cone. With mass, the turning paths fill the inside of the cone with waves.

Part 3 · Converging to the Dirac wave

Take the time t = 1 and cut it into N steps of size ε = 1/N. The sum on the grid, divided by the cell width 2ε, is compared with the exact answer. The exact answer has Bessel functions. When ε gets smaller, the curves meet.

Predict, then test: double N. By what factor does the largest error fall? Now switch the rule to unitary. Does the order change? Does the error get bigger or smaller?
What the numbers mean

The order p is the slope of log(error) against log(ε) for the four grids N, 2N, 4N, 8N. It is a least-squares fit. Error ∝ εp. For this scheme p is 1. The first step is placed at the cell next to the start, and this makes a shift of order ε. A shift of order ε in the position gives an error of order ε in a smooth wave.

The unitary rule has a smaller error constant here, but it has the same order. Its error comes from the same shift. The factor cos(mε) = 1 − (mε)²/2 changes the wave only at second order.

Part 4 · A wave packet on the checkerboard

Now start with a smooth packet and let the unitary rule move it, tick by tick. The packet cannot go faster than light. A packet with mass goes slower, with the group speed k/√(k²+m²). If the packet starts in one component only, it also shakes (Zitterbewegung).

Guide: what you are looking at

In one minute

Take a particle on a line. In each tick of a clock it jumps one cell to the left or to the right. It never stops, so it always moves at the speed of light. Now give it a mass in a very simple way: each time it turns around, its amplitude is multiplied by −iεm. Here ε is the step size and m is the mass. That is the whole rule.

Add the arrows of all zig-zag paths. When the steps get smaller and smaller, the sum becomes the exact propagator of the Dirac equation in one space dimension. This is a path integral for a particle with spin that you can count on a chessboard. Feynman found it, and Feynman and Hibbs set it as a problem in their book.

The page checks four things. (1) The recursion and the sum over all paths give the same number. (2) The number of paths with R turns follows a binomial formula. (3) The grid sum approaches the exact wave, with an error that falls in proportion to ε. (4) The exact wave obeys the Dirac equation.

Things to try

  1. Part 1: set the turn strength to 0.1 and press new path a few times.
    What happens and whyThe chain is almost one long arrow (R = 0 or R = 1). Every extra turn shrinks the arrow by the factor 0.1. So a path with many turns hardly matters. This is a small-mass expansion: the sum is a power series in m.
  2. Part 1: set the turn strength to 1.0 and the end point near the middle.
    What happens and whyNow long arrows come from paths with many turns, because there are very many of them (the binomials grow). Arrows with R = 0, 4, 8 point one way and arrows with R = 2, 6 point the other way, so they cancel each other in part. The sum is much smaller than the sum of the lengths. This is interference between paths.
  3. Part 2: set the mass to 0.
    What happens and whyOnly the edge of the light cone is lit, and only for "end right, start right" (or "end left, start left"). The particle never turns, so only one path exists. The two other components are exactly 0.
  4. Part 2: raise m, then raise N.
    What happens and whyWaves appear inside the cone. They have the shape J₀(m√(t²−x²)), so rings of equal phase follow the curves of constant proper time. Higher m makes finer rings. A finer grid (larger N) resolves them better; with a coarse grid the picture is only a rough copy.
  5. Part 2: switch between the plain and the unitary rule and read the norm.
    What happens and whyThe plain rule has a norm that grows by (1+ε²m²) at every tick, so the total probability is not conserved on the grid. The unitary rule uses cos(mε) and −i sin(mε) and keeps the norm at 1 exactly. When ε goes to 0, both rules give the same wave.
  6. Part 3: double N and read the error table.
    What happens and whyThe largest error falls by a factor of about 2. The fitted order is close to 1. The error is of first order in ε. In a log–log plot, the points lie on a straight line of slope 1.
  7. Part 4: set k = 0, start with the right component only, σ = 0.3, m = 3.
    What happens and whyThe zoomed plot shows a fast wiggle. Its period is π/ω, where ω is the energy of a rest particle (ω = m). The wiggle fades as the packet spreads. With a wide packet (σ ≫ 1/m) it is tiny.
  8. Part 4: use one speed, and compare the white line with the dashed line.
    What happens and whyThe two lines nearly match. The measured speed is an average over the packet, so it differs slightly from the formula for one k. A packet with large mass moves slowly. A packet with m small and k large moves close to the speed of light 1, and never faster.

The physics

The rule. Use units with c = ħ = 1. Cut time and space into steps ε. A path jumps ε to the right or to the left in each tick. Every path gets the weight (−iεm)R where R is the number of turns. The wave from the start to a point (x, t) is the sum over all paths. Because a path has a first and a last direction, the result is a 2×2 table Kend,start. The sum can be written as ΣR N(R) (−iεm)R, with N(R) the number of paths with R turns. This is a power series in the mass, so it is also a perturbation series: each turn is like one interaction with the mass.

The limit. One tick of the recursion says ψR(x,t+ε) − ψR(x−ε,t) = −iεm ψL(x−ε,t). Divide by ε and let ε go to 0: (∂t + ∂x) ψR = −i m ψL, and (∂t − ∂x) ψL = −i m ψR. This is the Dirac equation for a spinor with two components (right-moving and left-moving). The solution for a point source is the retarded propagator. Its components are Bessel functions inside the light cone and a sharp spike on the edge. The mass sets how fast the waves inside oscillate.

The sign and the cell. With the sign −iεm the sum obeys (∂t + ∂x)ψR = −imψL. With +iεm you get the same physics with m → −m (a different sign convention of the Dirac matrices). Only every second cell is reached at a given time, so the cell width in x is 2ε. This is why the grid sum is divided by 2ε. Then the sum is a density in x, as the propagator is.

Plain and unitary. The plain rule is not unitary on the grid. Every tick multiplies the norm by 1+ε²m². After N−1 ticks it is (1+ε²m²)N−1. This tends to 1 when ε goes to 0 with t = Nε fixed, but only like 1 + εm²t. The unitary variant puts cos(mε) on each step without a turn and −i sin(mε) on each turn. The norm is 1 at every tick. It is a quantum walk: a particle with a "coin" in the direction (right or left), and the coin is turned by an angle mε at every tick. The wave on the grid has the dispersion cos(ωε) = cos(mε) cos(kε), which tends to ω² = k² + m².

Packets. A packet made of waves eikx with k near k₀ moves with the group speed dω/dk = k/√(k²+m²). This is always less than 1 for m > 0. A packet that starts in one component only is a mix of positive-energy and negative-energy waves. The two parts beat with frequency 2ω. The mean position then wiggles. This is the Zitterbewegung. It has the size of one Compton wavelength 1/m. See Feynman Diagrams for the other, perturbative way of adding up paths.

Honest limits

The checkerboard works for one space dimension and one time dimension only. The simple rule does not extend to three space dimensions in this form. Real electrons live in 3+1 dimensions and have more than a "left" and "right" part. The grid is a trick for the sum; the physical wave is the limit ε → 0. At finite ε the plain rule is not unitary, and the error is first order. All errors on this page are for this chosen first-step convention. The errors are measured for |x| ≤ 0.9 t. On the light cone itself the wave has a sharp spike (a delta function) that a grid can only show as one bright cell. The mass is the only interaction: there is no field, no spin-flip beyond the turn, and no second particle. The packet in Part 4 uses a grid with ε = 0.05, so its speed differs from k/√(k²+m²) by a small amount that grows with k. Zitterbewegung is a feature of the equation for one particle. It is hard to observe in a real electron because it is very fast and very small. The words "path" and "turn" are pictures; only the sum has a meaning in an experiment.
