From Paths to Schrödinger

The path integral is a product of many short steps. Cut time into N slices. One slice is easy: a free step and a small kick from the potential. Do it N times and you get the wave. Here you build the sum from slices, you watch it converge, and you measure how fast.

How to use this room
  1. Part 1: set N = 2 and drag the single middle dot of the path. Watch the arrow eiS turn. Then raise N and see the polygon become a smooth path.
  2. Part 2: choose a potential and move N from 1 up to 256. Time goes down in the picture. Each row is one more step. Compare N = 1 with N = 64.
  3. Part 3: read the big number. It is the measured speed of convergence of the symmetric step, and 2 is the exact answer. Then look at the free particle line.
  4. Part 4: switch to imaginary time. The same steps now find the ground-state energy.

Part 1 · A sliced path

A path from the left point to the right point is a list of positions at N+1 times. The two ends are fixed. The N−1 dots in between are free. Each slice adds a small number to the action: the motion term (Δx)²/2ε and the potential term −ε V. The sum is SN. The path counts with the arrow eiSN. Here the potential is a harmonic trap, V = x²/2.

Part 2 · Integrate one slice at a time

Now the sum over all the dots is done for you. One slice does this to a wave: first the potential turns the phase of each point, e−iεV(x). Then the free step lets the wave spread. The free step is the exact free kernel, applied with a fast Fourier transform. Repeat N times. Each row of the picture is the wave after one more step. Time goes down. Brightness is |ψ|. Colour is the phase.

Part 3 · Converge

How fast does the error fall when N grows? Take the harmonic trap. The exact answer is the sum over oscillator states, which does not use slicing at all. For each N the page runs the slices and measures the distance to the exact wave. Two splittings are compared. Plain: potential step, then free step. The error falls like 1/N. Symmetric: half a potential step, free step, half a potential step. The error falls like 1/N².

Part 4 · Imaginary time: the same product, a different answer

Replace the time step ε by −iε. Now the steps do not turn phases. They damp. The free step becomes a smoothing step (diffusion). The potential step becomes e−εV. After many steps only the state with the lowest energy survives. Each step shrinks the norm by the factor e−εE. So the shrink rate gives the energy. In the harmonic trap the exact ground-state energy is ½. The shape of the surviving wave also has an error, and there the two splittings differ.

Guide: what you are looking at

In one minute

The path integral says: add one arrow (an amplitude) for every path. But "every path" is a lot. A computer cannot do that directly. The trick is to cut time into N small slices. A path is then a list of N+1 positions. For one slice the answer is simple: a free jump plus a small kick from the potential. The sum over all paths becomes N−1 ordinary integrals, one for each free dot.

This room shows that this really works. You build one sliced path by hand. You then do the integrals slice by slice, and you watch the wave appear. You measure the error and see it fall as N grows. At the end you get the wave of Schrödinger's equation back. The same steps in imaginary time find the ground state.

Things to try

  1. Part 1: set N = 2, kind "classical". Drag the middle dot up and down.
    What happens and whySN is smallest near the classical position, and it grows when you move the dot away. The classical path is a place where the action is stationary (it hardly changes). For the harmonic trap with T below π, it is even a minimum. Near that place the arrows of neighbouring paths point the same way, so they add up. Far from it the arrows turn fast and cancel.
  2. Part 1: choose "smooth wiggles" and move N from 2 to 64.
    What happens and whyThe polygon becomes the smooth curve. SN settles on one number, the action of the smooth path. The classical polygon error in the line below falls by a factor of 4 for each doubling of N. That is order 2.
  3. Part 1: choose "rough" and raise N.
    What happens and whySN keeps growing. A random walk has steps of size about √ε, so each motion term (Δx)²/2ε does not shrink. The sum grows like N. Typical paths in the path integral are not smooth. The sum still works because the arrows of such paths cancel almost completely. The smooth path is the exception that survives.
  4. Part 2: choose "free". Move N from 1 to 256.
    What happens and whyThe last row does not change at all (the error is about 10−15). One free step of time T is exactly N steps of time T/N. The free kernel composes exactly. The integral over each middle dot gives back the same kernel.
  5. Part 2: harmonic trap, plain split, N = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16.
    What happens and whyWith N = 1 the whole motion is one kick and one free spread. The result is wrong. The potential and the free motion do not commute: it matters which comes first. When the steps are short, the order matters less and less. The error falls like 1/N.
  6. Part 2: switch to the symmetric split.
    What happens and whyFor the same N the error is much smaller. Half a kick before and half after treat the two orders in a balanced way. The first error term cancels. The error falls like 1/N².
  7. Part 3: read the big number, then change T and the width.
    What happens and whyThe fitted order stays near 2 for the symmetric split and near 1 for the plain split. The size of the error changes with T and width, but the slope does not. The order is a property of the formula.
  8. Part 4: set ε = 0.5 and then ε = 0.05.
    What happens and whyThe energy estimate goes to ½ when ε goes to 0. Its error falls by 4 each time ε is halved (order 2), and it is the same for both splittings. This is no accident. The two step operators are the same up to a change of variables (V1/2), so they have the same decay rate. But the shape of the surviving wave is different. For the plain split the shape error falls only by 2 when ε is halved (order 1). For the symmetric split it falls by 4 (order 2).

The physics

The propagator K(x,T;x₀) takes the wave from time 0 to time T. It has the composition property: K(x,T;x₀) = ∫ K(x,T−t;y) K(y,t;x₀) dy. You can cut time in two, then in N pieces. For short time ε the kernel of a particle in a smooth potential is, to a good approximation, the free kernel times a phase from the potential. Put N of them in a chain and integrate over the N−1 middle points. This is the discrete path integral. The phase of a polygon path is its sliced action SN. In the limit N → ∞ the polygons fill the space of all paths, and SN becomes the action ∫ L dt.

The same chain is a recipe for a computer. The wave after one slice is ψ(x,t+ε) = ∫ Kε(x,y) ψ(y,t) dy. The free part of this integral is a convolution. It is simple in Fourier space: each wave number k just gets the phase e−iεk²/2. So one slice is: multiply by a phase in x-space, Fourier transform, multiply by a phase in k-space, transform back. This is the "split-step" method. It is the product formula of Trotter and Suzuki: e−iε(K+V) ≈ e−iεV e−iεK. The error per slice is of order ε², and N slices give an error of order ε = T/N. The symmetric form e−iεV/2 e−iεK e−iεV/2 has a smaller error per slice, ε³, so the total is of order ε². The first error term is a commutator-type term: it exists only because the kinetic and potential parts do not commute. For V = 0 there is no error at all.

In the limit the result is the solution of the Schrödinger equation. For the harmonic oscillator the exact answer is known (the Mehler kernel, or the sum over oscillator states). So the oscillator is a clean test: you can measure the error exactly. For ε → −iε (a Wick rotation) each step is real and damps the wave. High energies decay fastest, and the ground state stays.

Honest limits

The wave lives on a grid of 256 points in a periodic box of width 32. The free step is exact on this grid, but the wave must stay away from the edges (it would wrap around). The grid error is tiny for smooth packets, and the page does not measure it separately. The error curves are measured for one start packet in one trap, not for all cases. The orders 1 and 2 are asymptotic: for N = 1 or 2 the curves can look different. The rough-path option is a cartoon of how rough typical paths are, not a correct sample of the path-integral measure. The potentials in Part 2 are smooth, so the orders hold. A potential with a true jump would give slower convergence. In Part 4 the exact energy ½ is the answer for the continuous oscillator. The grid and the finite ε give small deviations, and the page shows them. One dimension, one particle, no spin, no measurement.
