The Restless Vacuum

Take a field at its lowest energy. Nothing is added, nothing can be taken away. It still shakes. Put two walls close together, and the shaking leaves a tiny pull between them.

What the numbers mean

The field is a chain of N sites. Each site has a value φi and is joined to its neighbours by springs. The energy is H = Σ ½πi² + ½(φi+1 − φi)² + ½m²φi². The mass m makes each site also feel a pull to zero. We use ħ = 1 and wave speed 1. The chain is a ring: it closes on itself, so the first and last sites are neighbours.

The chain splits into independent waves (normal modes). The wave with wave number k has frequency ωk = √(m² + 4 sin²(k/2)). In the ground state every wave is in the lowest level of its own oscillator. Its amplitude is a Gaussian random number with variance 1/(2ωk). This is exact quantum mechanics. It means the field value at one site has the variance ⟨φ²⟩ = (1/N) Σk 1/(2ωk).

A heavy field has large ωk in all modes. So 1/(2ω) is small and the jitter is smaller. A light field jitters more, mostly in its long waves. With m = 0 the longest wave (k = 0) has no restoring force. We leave it out.

The value of one site is a snapshot of a random number. Neighbouring sites are partly in step with each other, so averaging over w sites cuts the jitter, but only slowly. The exact spread of the average over w sites is computed from the same sum with an extra factor sin²(wk/2)/sin²(k/2).

Each wave also holds the energy ½ωk. The sum E₀ = Σ ½ωk is the energy of the vacuum. It is never zero.

Now put walls where φ = 0, a distance L apart. Only standing waves n = 1 … L−1 fit, with ωn = 2 sin(nπ/(2L)). The energy between the walls is E(L) = ½ Σ ωn = Σ sin(nπ/(2L)). The identity Σ sin(nx) = sin(Mx/2) sin((M+1)x/2) / sin(x/2) gives the closed form E(L) = ½ (cot(π/(4L)) − 1). Expand it for large L: E(L) = (2/π) L − ½ − π/(24L) + …. The first term grows with L: it is the bulk energy. The second term is a fixed surface energy of the walls. Neither depends on the gap in a way that pulls. What is left is −π/(24L), the Casimir energy. It gets more negative as L gets smaller. The force is −dE/dL = −π/(24L²). Negative means attractive: the walls are pulled together.

This is the lattice version of the Casimir effect. In real 3D electromagnetism, with metal plates, the force per area is −π²ħc/(240 L⁴). The idea is the same: leave out the waves that do not fit, and the energy depends on L.

Caution on the picture. Subtracting bulk and surface energy is a choice made in the sum. On the lattice it is clean, because the closed form is exact. In a real experiment the plates are real objects, and only the L-dependent force is measured.
