Feynman Diagrams for a Real Particle

One quantum particle on a line meets a bump in the potential. The exact answer is known. The same answer also comes as a sum of diagrams: no kick, one kick, two kicks, and so on. Add the diagrams and watch the sum close in on the exact value, or run away from it.

How to use this room
  1. Pick a potential and move strength and wave number k. The big numbers show the exact |t|² and the sum of the first N diagrams.
  2. Move orders N. In part 1 the arrows (one per diagram) are added head to tail. They should end on the green ring. In part 2 see how fast the error falls, or grows.
  3. Make the strength large or k small. The series stops working. In part 3 see this at all energies. In part 4 check the one case that has a closed formula.
New to this? Read the guide at the bottom of the page. Unknown words are explained in the glossary. This room is the sister of Feynman Diagrams From a Path Integral (a toy with one number) and of Light takes every path.

The set-up exactsimulation

A particle with ħ = m = 1 and energy E = k²/2 comes from the left as a plane wave eikx. It meets a potential V(x). Far to the right the wave is t·eikx and far to the left it is eikx + r·e−ikx. The numbers t and r are complex amplitudes. The chance to pass is T = |t|², and |t|² + |r|² = 1.

The exact value comes from matching the wave through the potential with a transfer matrix. It does not use the series. Negative strength means an attractive potential (a well). For smooth potentials the potential is cut into a row of thin kicks: this makes an error of about 5·10⁻⁸ (barrier) or 10⁻⁶ (Gaussian) for the series. simulation

1. Each diagram is one term exactcartoon

The wave function obeys ψ = eikx + ∫ G₀(x,x′) V(x′) ψ(x′) dx′, with the free propagator G₀(x,x′) = eik|x−x′|/(ik). Put the whole right side back into the integral again and again. You get one term per number of kicks. The term of order n has n factors of V and n factors of G₀. That is a Feynman diagram: a line, n vertices.

2. Closing in, or running away exactsimulation

The series is a geometric-type series. Each kick multiplies the wave by the operator K = G₀V. The terms fall like ρⁿ, where ρ is the largest size of an eigenvalue of K. The series converges if and only if ρ < 1. The code finds ρ by applying K many times (it is the growth rate per order). For a single delta spike it is exactly |g|/k.

3. All energies exactsimulationinterpretation

The plot shows the chance to pass, T(k) = |t|², for the potential and strength you chose. White: exact. Orange: first Born approximation, T ≈ 1 − |r₁|², where r₁ is the Fourier transform of V at 2k. Blue: the sum of N orders, |tN|². The dashed vertical line is your k.

The first Born approximation is good at large k, where the particle is fast and the kick is short. At small k it fails. The N-th order sum fails where ρ ≥ 1. Values above 1.4 are cut at the top edge. Resonances (peaks to T = 1) appear for the well and the pair of spikes. interpretation A resonance is the wave bouncing back and forth inside the potential. Born terms of high order describe exactly this bouncing, so the series needs many orders there.
Predict, then test: choose the square well (strength −3). At which k is the transmission exactly 1? Is the series for N = 30 good at this k? Move to k = 4 and compare.

4. The one case with a closed formula exact

For one delta spike V = g δ(x) the kick integral has no integration left: every order is the same number times the one before. With γ = g/(ik) = −ig/k the terms are tₙ = γⁿ and rₙ = γⁿ (n ≥ 1). The series is geometric with ratio γ. It sums to t = 1/(1 − γ) = 1/(1 + ig/k), and it converges only if |γ| = |g|/k < 1. The table uses the strength and k from the sliders above, always for a single spike, and compares the grid code with the formula.

Predict, then test: an attractive spike (g < 0) has one bound state, with energy −g²/2. The series for scattering at energy E converges when |g| < k, that is when E is larger than g²/2. For this attractive spike the limit sits exactly at the binding energy. Is that a coincidence, or the cause? Set g = −2 and move k across 2. Then try g = +2: the spike has no bound state. Guide: what you are looking at

In one minute

A particle meets a potential. The exact answer is a wave with an amplitude t for passing and r for bouncing back. You can also build this answer in steps. First the particle does not feel the potential at all (order 0). Then it is kicked once by the potential, and between the kick and the detector it moves freely (order 1). Then it is kicked twice (order 2), and so on. Each step is one Feynman diagram: a line for free motion, and a dot (a vertex) for each kick. Add all diagrams and you get the exact amplitude. This is perturbation theory.

The room lets you check this with numbers. You also see that the series can fail: if the potential is strong or the particle is slow, more kicks make the answer worse, not better.

Things to try

  1. Square barrier, V₀ = 1, k = 2. Move N from 0 up to 30.
    What happens and whyThe exact |t|² is 0.8913. The sum starts at 1 (no potential), jumps to 1.25 after one kick, and then settles. The error of t is 0.56, 0.21, 0.083, 0.0073 and 0.00012 after 0, 1, 2, 5 and 10 orders. Each order gains a factor of about ρ = 0.44. The arrows in part 1 spiral in on the green ring. Note that after one kick |t₀ + t₁|² = 1.25 is larger than 1: a probability above 1 is impossible, so a low-order sum is not a physical state. It only becomes one when enough orders are added.
  2. Single delta spike, g = 3, k = 2.
    What happens and whyHere ρ = |g|/k = 1.5. The exact |t|² is 0.3077. The terms grow like 1.5ⁿ. The sum gives |tN|² = 3.25 for N = 1, 47 for N = 5, 2300 for N = 10 and 2.5·10¹⁰ for N = 30. Each new diagram makes the answer worse. The exact amplitude 1/(1 + ig/k) is perfectly fine; only its expansion in g fails. This is a series in g/k, and it has radius of convergence 1 (the pole of 1/(1 − γ) sits at |γ| = 1).
  3. Single delta spike, g = −2. Move k from 3 down to 1.
    What happens and whyThe spike holds one bound state with energy −g²/2 = −2. At k = 3 the ratio is ρ = 2/3 and the series converges (error 3·10⁻⁶ at N = 30). At k = 1 it is ρ = 2 and the series diverges. The limit is k = |g|, that is E = g²/2: the series works when the scattering energy is larger than the binding energy. The bound state is not the cause: a repulsive spike with g = +2 has none, and its series fails at the same k = 2. In general the series fails whenever the potential is strong compared with the particle's energy (ρ ≥ 1), with or without a bound state.
  4. Pair of delta spikes, g = 1. Compare k = 2 and k = 1.
    What happens and whyFor one spike, g = 1 would give ρ = 0.5 at k = 2. For two spikes at distance 1 the rate is ρ = (|g|/k)·2·max(|cos(ka/2)|, |sin(ka/2)|), so ρ = 0.84 at k = 2 and ρ = 1.76 at k = 1. The wave bounces between the spikes, so there are more diagrams (2ⁿ ways to choose the spikes), and the convergence is slower. At k = 2 the exact |t|² is 0.9985 (almost a resonance), but the error is still 0.0025 after 30 orders. At k = 1 the series fails.
  5. Square well V₀ = −3. In part 3, find the peak where T = 1.
    What happens and whyInside the well the wave number is q = √(k² + 6). The wave passes with no reflection when q·w = nπ. For n = 1 and width 1 this gives k = √(π² − 6) = 1.967. The exact T there is 1.0000, but ρ = 1.34, so the Born series diverges at the resonance. At k = 4 the transmission is 0.975 and ρ = 0.46: the sum converges, and the error is 1.4·10⁻⁷ at N = 30. Resonances are exactly where multiple scattering is strong, and that is where Born sums fail first.
  6. In part 3, compare the orange line (first Born) and the exact line for the barrier V₀ = 1.
    What happens and whyAt k = 2 the first Born value is T ≈ 1 − |r₁|² = 0.948, the exact value is 0.891. At k = 4 the two lines are close. First Born is the lowest-order answer in V and it is good when V·w/k is small. It uses the Fourier transform of V at 2k: r₁ = (1/ik) ∫ V(x) e2ikx dx. The simple guess |1 + t₁|² is worse (1.25 here), because t₁ is purely imaginary and the guess breaks the rule |t|² + |r|² = 1. Using 1 − |r₁|² keeps probability in order.
  7. Choose the Gaussian bump and move the strength to −6 with k = 3.
    What happens and whyA smooth well has a bound state too. You see that ρ is larger than 1 and the arrow path leaves the plot. Try k = 6: the series converges again. At large k the factor G₀ = eik|x−x′|/(ik) is small (it falls like 1/k), so a fast particle needs only a few kicks. For any potential that is limited in size and range, ρ goes to zero when k goes to infinity.

The physics

The Schrödinger equation for a particle in a potential is (E − H₀)ψ = Vψ, where H₀ is the free part. The free part has a propagator G₀, which is the response of the free particle to a point source at x′. In one dimension G₀(x,x′) = eik|x−x′|/(ik): a wave that leaves x′ in both directions. In units ħ = m = 1 the energy is E = k²/2 and the factor is 1/(ik), not 1/k². This gives the Lippmann–Schwinger equation ψ = ψ₀ + G₀Vψ. Put ψ on the right again and again, and you get the Born series ψ = ψ₀ + G₀Vψ₀ + G₀VG₀Vψ₀ + …

This is the same bookkeeping as in the toy room, where the "field" had a single number. Each term is a diagram: the line is free propagation, the dot is one interaction with V. In path-integral language, G₀ is the sum over all free paths from x′ to x (see Every path is an arrow; see Double Slit Flow for a path sum with a wall). The sum over diagrams is then the sum over paths that are kicked n times. For a smooth potential each vertex position is integrated over the potential. For delta spikes the integral is replaced by a choice of spike. Light takes every path shows the free sum for light.

The order of a diagram is the number of vertices. A vertex carries one factor V. A line carries one factor G₀. The transmission amplitude is t = 1 + Σ tₙ, with tₙ = (1/ik) ∫ e−ikx V ψₙ₋₁ dx. Reflection is the same with e+ikx. The first-order term of r is the Fourier transform of V at 2k: the potential acts like a grating, and the reflected wave is the part of V with the right spatial period. This is the first Born approximation.

The series converges if and only if all eigenvalues of K = G₀V are smaller than 1 in size. For a delta spike it is |g|/k. For a weak or short potential or a fast particle it holds. For a strong potential, a slow particle, or a potential that supports a bound or resonant state it fails. The failure is a property of the expansion, not of the physics: the exact amplitude is smooth in the potential strength. This is the same kind of failure as for the φ⁴ series in the toy room, but here the cause is simple: the potential is strong compared with the particle's energy (ρ ≥ 1). A bound state is not needed. For an attractive delta spike the edge ρ = 1 sits exactly at the binding energy, but a repulsive spike (g = +3, k = 2: ρ = 1.5) or a barrier (V₀ = +5, k = 2: ρ ≈ 2.2) fails too, with no bound state. Compare: in the toy the terms grow like n! for any g; here they grow only like ρⁿ, and a small enough coupling always works.

Honest reading: a Feynman diagram is a term in an expansion. It does not show a particle that "really" jumps from kick to kick. A particle in a potential has one wave, not many paths of a ball.

Honest limits

This is one particle in one dimension, with fixed potential, no spin, non-relativistic. The potential does not move or react: there is no photon, no field, no creation of particles. In quantum field theory the diagrams also have loops (a closed circuit of lines with a free momentum to sum) and lines that run backward in time (antiparticles). Here a diagram is only a chain: line, vertex, line, vertex, with no branching. The exact answer for smooth potentials is computed from a fine row of kicks, which has an error of about 5·10⁻⁸ to 10⁻⁶. The estimate of ρ comes from many applications of K and it is accurate to about 10⁻³ when two eigenvalues have nearly equal size. The first Born |t|² uses 1 − |r₁|²; other choices exist. "The series diverges" does not mean "the physics is wrong"; the exact amplitude exists for all strengths.
