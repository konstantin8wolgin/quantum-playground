Pull Two Charges Apart

Hold a positive and a negative charge in a row of sites. Between them the electric field makes a tube, and the tube costs energy for every step. Pull harder, and the tube does not stretch for ever. The vacuum makes a new pair, and the string breaks. This is a toy world with one space dimension (the Schwinger model, 1+1D QED). It is a cousin of QCD, not QCD itself.

How to use this room
  1. Press "break it". Wait a moment for the numbers ("computing…"). Then slide the separation r from 2 up to its largest value. Watch the blue curve on the right. Does it keep rising?
  2. Press "q = 0.5" and slide r again. This time the string does not break.
  3. Press "strong coupling (w = 0)". The big number must match J·q². Then raise hopping w slowly and see what happens to it.

What the numbers mean

Fermions sit on a chain of N sites (staggered fermions). In 1D the electric field has no freedom of its own. Gauss's law fixes it from the charges: the field on link n is the total charge to its left, Ln = Σk≤n ρk. So the gauge field can be removed, and one gets a spin chain: H = −w Σ (σ⁺nσ⁻n+1 + h.c.) + (m/2) Σ (−1)n σzn + J Σ Ln². The first term lets a charge hop. The second is the fermion mass. The third is the energy of the electric field, J per unit of field squared. Here J = 1 sets the unit of energy.

Two fixed charges +q and −q add q to the field on every link between them. In the strong-coupling limit w = 0 nothing hops. The cheapest state has no dynamical charge, so the field on r links is q, and the energy is exactly V(r) = J q² r. That is a linear potential, a string with tension σ = J q². (This is the same picture as the area law in the Wilson-loop room.)

With hopping, the vacuum can make a pair of dynamical charges. A pair costs about 2 m plus some hopping energy. A pair placed next to the fixed charges cancels their field, so the tube is cut. When that costs less than the string it replaces, the ground state switches. V(r) then stops rising. It flattens to a plateau, the energy of two fixed charges, each screened by a dynamical one. This is "string breaking". It happens in real QCD, where the pair is a quark and an antiquark and the pieces are two mesons.

If q is a whole number, the new charges can cancel the field fully. If q = 0.5, a new unit charge turns +0.5 into −0.5. The field is just as large as before. Energy is not saved, so the string never breaks. A fractional external charge stays confined.

The plateau is not 2 × the meson mass. The meson mass M (the lowest excitation of the empty chain, shown below) is a different object: a light pair. The plateau is two fixed charges, each bound to one light fermion, so it is two "heavy-light" masses. We show the plateau as computed from the data, and M for comparison.

Limits: N is small (8 to 14) and the chain has open ends, so V(r) bends up at the largest r. The two charges must sit on the same sublattice (r even); on the other sublattice the staggering of the lattice gives a zig-zag, which is a lattice effect. We keep J = 1 and vary w, which is the same as changing the lattice spacing.

Guide: what you are looking at

In one minute

Take two opposite electric charges and pull them apart. In normal 3D space the force gets weaker with distance. But in a world with only one space dimension, the electric field cannot spread out. It stays a thin tube between the charges. The tube holds energy for each step of its length. So the energy grows in proportion to the distance, like a stretched rubber band. A band like this is a "string".

A real string snaps if you pull it far enough. Here it snaps in a quantum way. The energy of the tube becomes larger than the energy that it costs to make a new pair: a charge and an opposite charge from the vacuum. The new charges sit at the two ends of the tube and cancel the fixed charges. The tube is gone. Two separate pairs remain, and each pair is neutral. This is why nobody sees a free quark: when you pull a quark away, the string between quarks breaks and makes new quarks.

The world in this room has one space dimension and one time dimension. It is called the Schwinger model, or 1+1D QED. It is a small, solvable cousin of QCD, not QCD itself.

Things to try

  1. Press "break it" (q = 1, m = 0.5, w = 1). Slide r from 2 to the largest value. Where does the line stop rising?
    What happens and whyWith N = 12 the values are about 1.68, 2.72, 2.86 and 2.97 for r = 2, 4, 6, 8. The curve rises at first (the slope is about 0.5 per site) and then flattens (the slope drops below 0.1). The string breaks near r = 4. Look at the left picture for r = 8. The tube in the middle is very thin (the field is below 0.2 on each link). Blue halos sit next to the orange +q, and orange halos sit next to the blue −q. Near each fixed charge the made charge adds up to about 0.9 with the opposite sign: it screens that fixed charge. (The sum is spread over the site and its two neighbours.) For a longer string it is cheaper to make a pair than to pay for more tube.
  2. Press "q = 0.5". Slide r. Will the string break?
    What happens and whyNo. V rises on a nearly straight line (0.43, 0.84, 1.24, 1.66). The vacuum can only make whole charges. A whole charge changes the field from +0.5 to −0.5. The field is as large as before, so the energy is the same, and nothing is saved. A fractional charge stays confined for ever. A charge of 1 changes the field from +1 to 0, and that saves energy. In this toy, a string breaks only when q is more than 0.5.
  3. Press "strong coupling (w = 0)". Compare the big number with J·q². Then raise w slowly.
    What happens and whyAt w = 0 (and m = 2) nothing hops. The two numbers agree to about 10−15. The tube holds a field of exactly q on each link, so V = J·q²·r. When you raise w, σ falls below J·q². The string gets cheaper. Hopping lets the vacuum fluctuate, and its small pairs partly screen the field. (With m = 0.5, w = 1, the value is about 0.84.)
  4. Press "break it", then raise the fermion mass m to 3.
    What happens and whyV now rises all the way (about 1.98, 3.96, 5.94, then 7.39 at r = 8). The break moves out to a longer string: the curve is almost straight up to r = 6 and only starts to bend at r = 8. A heavy particle is expensive to make, so the vacuum waits until the tube holds more energy.
  5. Press "strong coupling (w = 0)" and lower m to 0.2.
    What happens and whyThe big number is now lower than J·q². The label below it tells you why. When a pair is cheap (2m is less than the energy saved), the vacuum makes pairs even at r = 2. The exact value J·q² holds only when the fermions are heavy enough. For q = 1, this means m of about 0.5 or more.
  6. Change the number of sites N (8, 10, 12, 14).
    What happens and whyThe curve changes a little. A larger N gives more points on the plot and a longer string. The chain has open ends and is small, so the last points bend upward. You are seeing an edge effect of the small chain, not new physics.

The physics

Electric field has no freedom of its own in one space dimension. Gauss's law fixes it: the field on a link equals the total charge to the left of it. So a charge pair always has a constant field between them, whatever the distance. A constant field has an energy that grows with the length. This is confinement in the simplest form, with a string tension σ = J·q². The room Phases around a loop shows the same law as an area law.

The charged particles are fermions on a lattice. The trick of "staggered" fermions puts one kind of particle on every site. A site on an even position holds charge 0 or +1. A site on an odd position holds charge 0 or −1. The computer keeps the whole quantum state of N sites (it has 2N possible patterns, and only the neutral ones count). It finds the lowest-energy state, the ground state, with the Lanczos method. This is exact diagonalisation. There is no random sampling and no Monte Carlo noise.

The new charges come from the vacuum. In quantum field theory the vacuum is not empty: it holds pairs that can appear when there is energy to pay for them. Here the ground state itself is a mix of the "no pair" and "pair" patterns. When the string is long, the "pair" pattern gets the larger weight. This is the same superposition idea as in the other rooms.

In QCD the same thing happens. The tube is a tube of colour field, the fixed charges are heavy quarks, and the pieces after the break are two mesons. Lattice QCD calculations have seen this effect. Here the field has one component only, and space has one dimension. In QCD the field has three colours (SU(3)) and space has three dimensions. See Three colours.

The math

Show the formulas
ρk = ( σzk + (−1)k ) / 2 The charge on site k. The spin σz = ±1 says whether the site is filled. The term (−1)k is the staggering. Ln = Σk ≤ n ρk + εn Gauss's law in 1D. The field on link n is the charge to the left, plus the field εn of the fixed charges (equal to q on the r links between them). H = −w Σ (σ⁺nσ⁻n+1 + h.c.) + (m/2) Σ (−1)n σzn + J Σ Ln² Hopping, mass and field energy. "h.c." means the reverse hop. J = 1 sets the unit of energy. w = 0:   V(r) = J q² r With no hopping, the cheapest state has no dynamic charge. The field is q on r links, so the energy is J q² per link. This is exact. J q²   →   J (1 − q)²   on each link that a new unit charge cancels A new charge of size 1 changes the field on a link from q to q − 1. It saves energy only if (1 − q)² < q², that is, q > ½. At q = ½ it saves nothing. breaking pays when   J q² r > 2m + J A rough rule for q = 1 and w = 0. A pair costs about 2m. One link keeps a field of 1, because the two fixed charges sit on the same sublattice. For m = 2 this gives r > 2.5: V(4) = 4, V(6) = 5. V(r) = E0(r) − E0(no charges) The energy that the fixed charges add to the ground state, found from the Lanczos method.

Honest limits

The chain is tiny: N is 8 to 14 and the ends are open. So V(r) has only two to four points, the "plateau" is only the last of them, and the curve bends up at the largest r. This is exact for this small chain, but it is not the limit of a long chain. The fixed charges are put in by hand, and r is even so that both sit on the same sublattice. Odd r gives a zig-zag (a lattice effect). The check at w = 0 holds only when the mass is large enough that a pair does not pay at r = 2. The fractional charge q = 0.5 is a toy: in QCD the colour charge of a heavy quark is a fixed whole unit. And this is 1+1D QED: it has one kind of field and one space dimension, so it is not QCD.
