Order Matters

Ask a quantum system two questions. Swap the order, and you get different answers. Three views of one fact: spin magnets, the rule x·p ≠ p·x, and the map of a state in phase space.

What the numbers mean

An observable is a question you can ask. "Is the spin up or down along z?" is one question. "Where is the particle?" is another. Each question has a matrix or an operator A. The possible answers are its eigenvalues.

The answer is random. The average is not. For a state ψ, the chance of an answer a is |⟨a|ψ⟩|² (the Born rule). The average of many answers is ⟨A⟩ = ⟨ψ|A|ψ⟩. For spin along a magnet axis n = (sin φ, 0, cos φ) the chance of "up" is (1 + r·n)/2, where r is the direction of the spin (the Bloch vector). After the answer, the state is that answer: r = ±n.

Two questions that do not commute disturb each other. The z question and the x question have [σx, σz] = σxσz − σzσx = −2iσy ≠ 0. After you ask x, the z answer is gone: the chance of z+ is 50 % again. In the preset, the last two detectors get 25 % each of the particles (50 % of those that arrive). With two z magnets in a row, nothing is lost, because z commutes with itself.

For position and momentum, p = −iħ d/dx and [x, p] = xp − px = iħ. Panel 2 does this on a grid with finite differences: the difference is iψ up to a small grid error (it is shown in the readout). This one rule gives the uncertainty relation Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2. A coherent state sits exactly at 1/2.

The Wigner function. W(x,p) = (1/π) ∫ ψ*(x+y) ψ(x−y) e2ipy dy. Its integral over p is the probability to find x. Its integral over x is the probability to find p. Its total integral is 1. But W itself can be negative: negative W means no classical probability can explain the state. The Fock 1 state has W(0,0) = −1/π ≈ −0.318. The blue stripes of the cat state are the interference of its two parts.
