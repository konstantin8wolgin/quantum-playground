Watching the Slits

Two slits make stripes. Now put a marker on the electron that records which slit it used. How good must the marker be to kill the stripes? And can you bring the stripes back?

How to use this room
  1. Wait until dots build up on the screen (about 15 seconds). With "how well we watch" at 0°, you see full stripes.
  2. Move the how well we watch slider to 90°, then to 180°. The stripes fade. The big number below shows the stripe contrast V and the "path knowledge" D. Their squares always add to 1.
  3. Set the slider to 180° and tick quantum eraser. The dots get two colours. Each colour alone shows stripes. All dots together show none.

What the numbers mean

The marker is a small spin that starts up. A "coil" in the lower slit turns it by θ. An electron through the upper slit leaves the marker as χ₁ (up). An electron through the lower slit leaves it as χ₂ (turned by θ). The overlap ⟨χ₁|χ₂⟩ = cos(θ/2) tells how alike the two marker states are. With θ = 0 they are the same: the marker holds no information. With θ = 180° they are orthogonal: one look at the marker tells the slit.

Contrast V = (Pmax − Pmin)/(Pmax + Pmin) is measured on the exact curve near the centre. The code takes the central maximum, the nearest minimum and the next maximum, and corrects for the slow single-slit envelope. The predicted value is |⟨χ₁|χ₂⟩|. They agree to better than 0.001.

D = √(1 − |⟨χ₁|χ₂⟩|²) is how well the marker tells the two slits apart (best guess of the slit is right with probability ½(1 + D)). For a pure marker state, V² + D² = 1. For a mixed marker state, only V² + D² ≤ 1 holds.

"From dots" is a second estimate: the code averages the local interference ratio over all dots and compares it to the exact table. It has a statistical error that falls like 1/√n.

Guide: what you are looking at

In one minute

In Double Slit Flow, electrons make stripes on a screen after two slits. The stripes need one thing: nothing in the world may record which slit the electron used. Here we put a record in the world, a small marker, and we look at what happens to the stripes.

The rule is simple. If the marker ends in the same state for both slits, the record is empty, and the stripes are full. If the marker ends in two different states, then the two ways are no longer the same final situation, and the interference gets weaker. If the two marker states are orthogonal, the stripes are gone. The stripes fade smoothly in between. More knowledge of the path means less contrast, never more.

The word "watching" here means any physical record of the path. It does not need a person, a mind, or a screen with a reader. A single scattered photon that carries the information away is enough. There is no "electron that knows it is watched". There is an entanglement between the electron and the marker, and the arrows (amplitudes) for the two slits can only add when the marker does not tell them apart.

The last part is the quantum eraser. If the marker is fully on, the stripes are gone. But you can still read the marker in a sideways way, which does not tell the slit. Sort the dots by that sideways result and each pile shows stripes again. One pile has stripes, the other has "anti-stripes". The sum is the pattern without stripes, as before. Nothing was undone in the electrons: only your sorting of them changed.

Things to try

  1. Move θ from 0° to 180° slowly.
    What happens and whyThe stripes fade and the dark gaps fill with dots. At 180° the pattern is a smooth hump. The contrast follows |cos(θ/2)|: at 90° it is 0.71, not 0.5. The marker is a quantum state, so the "half" step of knowing is not half the contrast. What does add up is V² + D² = 1.
  2. Set θ = 90° and read V and D.
    What happens and whyBoth are about 0.71. V² = 0.5 and D² = 0.5. You get a half-good stripe pattern and a half-good guess of the slit. You can not have both fully. This is Bohr's complementarity, in the exact form of Englert (1996).
  3. Set θ = 180°. Dots show no stripes. Now tick quantum eraser.
    What happens and whyThe dots get two colours. Look at the teal dots alone: they sit in stripes. The pink dots sit in the gaps of the teal ones. The stripes were never lost: they were hidden in a mix of two patterns. The marker, read sideways, tells you which pile each electron belongs to. P₊ + P₋ is the smooth pattern again.
  4. Untick colour dots by eraser result.
    What happens and whyYou see the sum of both piles: a smooth hump. Reading the marker did not change where the electrons land. Only the sorting changes what you can see. So the eraser can not send a message back in time: someone who has only the unsorted dots sees no stripes, whatever you decide to do with the marker.
  5. Make the slit distance d smaller or larger.
    What happens and whyThe stripes get wider (small d) or closer (large d). The stripe distance is about λL/d. The contrast V does not depend on d: it depends only on how alike the two marker states are.

The physics

Each slit gives a wave at the screen. The room computes it as a sum over straight paths from every point in the slit to the screen point (see Every path is an arrow). Call the result ψ₁(y) for the upper slit and ψ₂(y) for the lower. This is the same orange-curve method as in Double Slit Flow, but with a finite slit width and with the exact path length, not the far-field formula.

Without a marker the electron is in a superposition (ψ₁ + ψ₂)/√2. With a marker, the joint state of electron and marker is

(ψ₁(y) |χ₁⟩ + ψ₂(y) |χ₂⟩) / √2

This is an entangled state when χ₁ and χ₂ differ. A detector at the screen only sees the electron. For this detector the marker is "traced out": we add the probabilities for the marker, not the arrows. The cross term is then multiplied by ⟨χ₁|χ₂⟩, the overlap of the two marker states. When the overlap is 0, the cross term is 0 and no stripes remain. This is what many people call decoherence of the path: the marker carries the phase information away.

The eraser measures the marker in the basis |±⟩ = (|χ₁⟩ ± |χ₂⟩)/√2 (for θ = 180°). The result "+" leaves the electron in the state ψ₁ + ψ₂, with probability density ¼|ψ₁ + ψ₂|². The result "−" gives ¼|ψ₁ − ψ₂|². Each of these is a full stripe pattern, shifted by half a stripe against the other. Their sum has no stripes. This is the experiment of Scully, Englert and Walther (1991), and it has been done with photons (for example Walborn et al. 2002).

Honest limits

The marker is an ideal two-level spin, and its states are pure. A real marker (a photon, an air molecule) has many states and is partly mixed; then V² + D² can be less than 1. The slits are equal and the source is a plane wave, so the pattern is mirror-symmetric. The wave is a scalar in two dimensions; edge effects of the slits are not included. The screen window is finite, so the probabilities are normalised inside the window. The electron is not really "told" a slit: no model here says which slit an electron used. The dots are random numbers from a computer, not a simulation of a detector. The eraser here is measured at once with the electron; the "delayed choice" version gives the same statistics but is not simulated. The set-up picture is a cartoon.
