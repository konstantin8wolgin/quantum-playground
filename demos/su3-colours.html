Three Colours

A quark has three colour amplitudes. SU(3) turns them into each other. Here you can turn them by hand, see that the order of turns matters, and see the patterns the same maths makes for the light quarks u, d and s.

How big is SU(3)? A 3×3 complex matrix has 9 free numbers. That group is called U(3), and it has 9 dimensions. SU(3) is the part with determinant 1. That one rule removes one dimension, so SU(3) has 8 dimensions. Each dimension has one generator: the eight Gell-Mann matrices λ1 … λ8.

One maths, two uses. The same SU(3) maths appears twice in nature. Colour SU(3) is an exact gauge symmetry of the strong force. Its 8 generators are the 8 gluons. Flavour SU(3) of the quarks u, d, s is only an approximate symmetry (the "eightfold way"). The s quark is heavier than u and d, so the patterns are slightly bent. Panels 1 and 2 are colour. Panel 3 is flavour. Do not mix them up: a "red" quark and an "up" quark are different labels.

Each pair of colours is a copy of SU(2): it behaves like a spin ½. The dot is the "spin direction" of that pair (x across, z up; a bright dot points toward you in y). Its distance from the centre is how much of the state sits in the pair. Turn λ1 and watch the red–green dot move. A full 360° turn of a pair flips the sign of its two arrows: see The Spinor Flag.
What the numbers mean

Generators. The eight Gell-Mann matrices λ1 … λ8 are 3×3 matrices. They are Hermitian (equal to their own conjugate transpose) and have trace 0. They are the SU(3) version of the three Pauli matrices. λ1, λ2, λ3 are the Pauli matrices placed in the red–green corner. λ4, λ5 do the same for red–blue. λ6, λ7 do the same for green–blue. λ8 = diag(1, 1, −2)/√3 is the one new matrix.

Exponential map. A generator makes a turn. The matrix U = exp(i·t·λk/2) is the turn by amount t. It is unitary (it keeps Σ|c|² = 1) and has determinant 1, because λk has trace 0. In this page the exponential comes from a Taylor series with scaling and squaring. The half angle t/2 is the same half angle as in the spinor room: for λ1 the state returns to itself only after t = 4π, and after t = 2π it carries a minus sign.

Why 8 gluons. Gluons are the "force arrows" of colour. There is one for each generator. 3×3 = 9 numbers, minus 1 for the determinant rule, gives 8. Six gluons change colour (red-to-green and so on). Two do not change colour, and correspond to λ3 and λ8. (The 9th combination, the colour singlet, would be a ninth gluon that does not exist in nature.)

The torus. λ3 and λ8 commute. They only turn phases. Together they make a 2-dimensional "torus" inside SU(3): two independent phases, with the third fixed by determinant 1. The other six generators mix colours.

Link to the story "nature adds up phases". In U(1) the arrow is one phase. In SU(2) it is a 2×2 rotation of two arrows. In SU(3) it is a 3×3 rotation of three arrows. A colour charge moving through a gluon field picks up such a 3×3 turn along its path. Turns in SU(3) do not commute, so the order of the path steps matters. Panel 2 shows this.

What the numbers mean

For two generators A and B, the turns commute only if the generators commute. The commutator [λ1/2, λ2/2] = i·λ3/2 means that a λ1 turn then a λ2 turn differs from the reverse by a small λ3 turn. The number in front, the structure constant f123, equals 1. The structure constants fabc define the group completely. The check f458 = √3/2 shows where λ8 enters.

The unitarity check applies 1000 random turns (random generator, random angle) to a random state. The sum Σ|c|² must stay 1. Rounding errors stay below 10⁻¹².

What the numbers mean

Nine pairs, eight plus one. A quark and an antiquark give 3 × 3 = 9 combinations. Their weights add. Seven different points appear: six corners of a hexagon (each hit once) and the centre (hit three times: u ū, d d̄, s s̄). Counting states, 3 ⊗ 3̄ = 8 ⊕ 1. The octet is the hexagon with two states at the centre. The singlet is the third centre state. In nature these three centre states are the π⁰, the η and the η′ (the η and η′ are mixtures of the "octet" and "singlet" states). The six corners are π⁺, π⁻, K⁺, K⁰, K̄⁰, K⁻. Together the nine mesons are the nonet.

Root vectors. A root is the difference of two quark weights, for example u − d = (1, 0). There are six. Each one belongs to a ladder operator that turns one quark flavour into another (for example d → u). Applied to a state, it moves the weight by the root, or gives zero if no state exists at the target. The pairs u–d, u–s and d–s belong to λ1, λ2 and λ4, λ5 and λ6, λ7. These are the flavour twins of the colour moves in Panel 1.

Limits. This is flavour SU(3). It is only approximate, because the quark masses differ. The real π⁰, η, η′ mix. The ladder here moves one highlighted weight: it does not track the full state vector. A decuplet triangle of baryons (3 ⊗ 3 ⊗ 3) is not drawn in this room.
