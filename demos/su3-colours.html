Three Colours

A quark has three colour amplitudes. SU(3) turns them into each other. Here you can turn them by hand, see that the order of turns matters, and see the patterns the same maths makes for the light quarks u, d and s.

How to use this room
  1. Panel 1: the state starts as pure red. Press and hold + under λ1. The red arrow shrinks and the green arrow grows. Release when the red arrow is gone.
  2. Press the buttons of the other groups and watch the three arrows and the three small dots change. Use mix of all three or random for a richer start.
  3. Panel 2: move the amount t slider and compare the first two rows. Panel 3: pick a quark and an antiquark, and see which meson corner the pair lands on.
New to this? Read the guide at the bottom of the page. Unknown words are explained in the glossary.

How big is SU(3)? A 3×3 complex matrix has 9 free numbers. That group is called U(3), and it has 9 dimensions. SU(3) is the part with determinant 1. That one rule removes one dimension, so SU(3) has 8 dimensions. Each dimension has one generator: the eight Gell-Mann matrices λ1 … λ8.

One maths, two uses. The same SU(3) maths appears twice in nature. Colour SU(3) is an exact gauge symmetry of the strong force. Its 8 generators are the 8 gluons. Flavour SU(3) of the quarks u, d, s is only an approximate symmetry (the "eightfold way"). The s quark is heavier than u and d, so the patterns are slightly bent. Panels 1 and 2 are colour. Panel 3 is flavour. Do not mix them up: a "red" quark and an "up" quark are different labels.

What the numbers mean

Generators. The eight Gell-Mann matrices λ1 … λ8 are 3×3 matrices. They are Hermitian (equal to their own conjugate transpose) and have trace 0. They are the SU(3) version of the three Pauli matrices. λ1, λ2, λ3 are the Pauli matrices placed in the red–green corner. λ4, λ5 do the same for red–blue. λ6, λ7 do the same for green–blue. λ8 = diag(1, 1, −2)/√3 is the one new matrix.

Exponential map. A generator makes a turn. The matrix U = exp(i·t·λk/2) is the turn by amount t. It is unitary (it keeps Σ|c|² = 1) and has determinant 1, because λk has trace 0. In this page the exponential comes from a Taylor series with scaling and squaring. The half angle t/2 is the same half angle as in the spinor room: for λ1 the state returns to itself only after t = 4π, and after t = 2π it carries a minus sign.

Why 8 gluons. Gluons are the "force arrows" of colour. There is one for each generator. 3×3 = 9 numbers, minus 1 for the determinant rule, gives 8. Six gluons change colour (red-to-green and so on). Two do not change colour, and correspond to λ3 and λ8. (The 9th combination, the colour singlet, would be a ninth gluon that does not exist in nature.)

The torus. λ3 and λ8 commute. They only turn phases. Together they make a 2-dimensional "torus" inside SU(3): two independent phases, with the third fixed by determinant 1. The other six generators mix colours.

Link to the story "nature adds up phases". In U(1) the arrow is one phase. In SU(2) it is a 2×2 rotation of two arrows. In SU(3) it is a 3×3 rotation of three arrows. A colour charge moving through a gluon field picks up such a 3×3 turn along its path. Turns in SU(3) do not commute, so the order of the path steps matters. Panel 2 shows this.

What the numbers mean

For two generators A and B, the turns commute only if the generators commute. The commutator [λ1/2, λ2/2] = i·λ3/2 means that a λ1 turn then a λ2 turn differs from the reverse by a small λ3 turn. The number in front, the structure constant f123, equals 1. The structure constants fabc define the group completely. The check f458 = √3/2 shows where λ8 enters.

The unitarity check applies 1000 random turns (random generator, random angle) to a random state. The sum Σ|c|² must stay 1. Rounding errors stay below 10⁻¹².

What the numbers mean

Nine pairs, eight plus one. A quark and an antiquark give 3 × 3 = 9 combinations. Their weights add. Seven different points appear: six corners of a hexagon (each hit once) and the centre (hit three times: u ū, d d̄, s s̄). Counting states, 3 ⊗ 3̄ = 8 ⊕ 1. The octet is the hexagon with two states at the centre. The singlet is the third centre state. In nature these three centre states are the π⁰, the η and the η′ (the η and η′ are mixtures of the "octet" and "singlet" states). The six corners are π⁺, π⁻, K⁺, K⁰, K̄⁰, K⁻. Together the nine mesons are the nonet.

Root vectors. A root is the difference of two quark weights, for example u − d = (1, 0). There are six. Each one belongs to a ladder operator that turns one quark flavour into another (for example d → u). Applied to a state, it moves the weight by the root, or gives zero if no state exists at the target. The pairs u–d, u–s and d–s belong to λ1, λ2 and λ4, λ5 and λ6, λ7. These are the flavour twins of the colour moves in Panel 1.

Limits. This is flavour SU(3). It is only approximate, because the quark masses differ. The real π⁰, η, η′ mix. The ladder here moves one highlighted weight: it does not track the full state vector. A decuplet triangle of baryons (3 ⊗ 3 ⊗ 3) is not drawn in this room.

Guide: what you are looking at

In one minute

A quark carries a kind of charge that comes in three types. Physicists call them red, green and blue, but they are only names. They have nothing to do with the colours of light. This charge is the colour charge. It is the source of the strong force, which holds the quarks together inside protons and neutrons. A leftover of the same force holds the nucleus together.

Quantum mechanics lets a quark be in a mix of the three types. The mix is three amplitudes: one arrow for red, one for green and one for blue. The strong force turns these arrows into each other. All the allowed turns form a group. It is called SU(3), and it has 8 independent ways of turning, one for each generator.

This room lets you do the turns with your hands. You will see three things. First, the turns never change the total: the three squared lengths always add to 1. Second, the order of the turns matters. Third, the same maths, used for the light quarks u, d and s, draws the hexagon of the lightest mesons.

Things to try

  1. Start from pure red. Hold λ1 "+" until the state is pure green. Then hold λ4. Does blue appear?
    What happens and whyNo blue appears. λ4 only mixes red with blue. After the first turn there is no red any more, so λ4 has nothing to mix. You need λ6 or λ7 (green with blue) to reach blue. The arrow lengths of the other colours do not change.
  2. Now hold λ3 or λ8. Do the sizes of the arrows change, or only the angles?
    What happens and whyOnly the angles change. λ3 and λ8 are diagonal matrices, so they only multiply each amplitude by a phase. The three lengths and the colour swatch stay the same. Together these two make the "torus" of pure phase turns.
  3. Hold λ1 "+" for about four seconds (a full turn, t = 2π). Watch the red and green arrows.
    What happens and whyBoth arrows come back to their start but with the opposite sign: they point the other way. The blue arrow is unchanged, because λ1 does not touch blue. You need t = 4π to be back at the start. This is the half angle t/2 that you meet in The Spinor Flag.
  4. Panel 2: set t to a very small value. Is the difference big or small? Then double t.
    What happens and whyThe difference is small. When you double t, a generic state gives a difference four times larger: it grows as t². Use "mix of all three" in Panel 1 to see this. The size is about (t²/4)·√(|g|² + |b|²). There is one special case. The default start, pure red, gives a much smaller difference that grows as t³: doubling t makes it eight times larger. The reason: the leading term comes from the commutator of λ1 and λ4, which is proportional to λ7, and λ7 does nothing to a pure red state. So the order matters less for red.
  5. Panel 2: set t = 180° with the "mix of all three" start.
    What happens and whyThe difference is large. The two orders now end in clearly different states. Order matters because the generators do not commute: [λ1, λ4] is not zero.
  6. Panel 3: pick u and anti-u. Where does the sum land?
    What happens and whyOn the centre of the hexagon, at (0, 0). The weight of the antiquark is the exact opposite of the quark's, so the sum is zero. The centre holds three states: u ū, d d̄ and s s̄. In nature they mix into π⁰, η and η′.
  7. Panel 3: pick u and anti-s. Which corner is it?
    What happens and whyThe corner K⁺ at (½, 1), top right. u has (½, ⅓) and anti-s has (0, +⅔). The sum is (½, 1). The electric charge Q = I3 + Y/2 is 1, as for K⁺. How many pairs land on the centre? Three: u ū, d d̄ and s s̄. The other six pairs land on the six corners, one each.
  8. Panel 3: from the π⁺ corner, press the ladder buttons.
    What happens and whyEach green arrow moves the state by one root, for example d→u moves by (1, 0). If the arrow would leave the hexagon there is no state at the end, and the ladder gives zero. The six roots are the differences between quark weights.

The physics

Colour is exact. In the strong force, the choice of which axis is "red" is only a convention. You may turn the three colour arrows by any SU(3) matrix, and the laws do not change. This is a gauge symmetry. This symmetry is the reason why the force exists: a symmetry that may differ from point to point needs a gauge field to carry the information. This field has 8 components, one for each generator. Its quanta are the 8 gluons. This theory is quantum chromodynamics (QCD).

Why 8. A 3 × 3 unitary matrix has 9 free numbers. The rule "determinant 1" removes one. So SU(3) has 8 dimensions. In other rooms, you saw U(1) (one arrow, 1 generator) and SU(2) (two arrows, 3 generators), for example in The Spinor Flag. SU(3) is the next step.

Order matters. The turns of U(1) commute: the order does not matter. The turns of SU(2) and SU(3) do not. The size of the effect is measured by the commutator. This is also why the gluons carry colour themselves, and why they interact with each other. That is the basis of confinement: no free quark is seen. The room Phases Around a Loop shows a simple picture of this.

Flavour is not exact. Panel 3 uses the same group in a different role. The quarks u, d and s have nearly equal masses compared with the other quarks. So the laws are almost symmetric when you turn u, d and s into each other. This is the "eightfold way" of Gell-Mann and Ne'eman. It is only approximate, because the s quark is heavier. The hexagon and its centre are the result of the group: a quark and an antiquark make 3 × 3 = 9 states, which split into 8 + 1.

Honest limits

The colour state here is a global turn of three amplitudes, with no gauge field and no force. You see the group, not QCD. A single coloured quark cannot exist alone in nature (confinement), so Panel 1 shows a state that you cannot make in a laboratory. The colour swatch is a cartoon. Panel 3 is flavour SU(3), which is only approximate. The states π⁰, η and η′ are not simple sums of u ū, d d̄ and s s̄: they mix. The ladder moves one highlighted weight, not a full quantum state. Baryons (three quarks, the "decuplet" triangle) are not drawn.
