Paths That Wind Around a Ring

A particle on a ring. Its paths go round the ring n times, and n sorts them into sectors. A flux through the ring gives every path in sector n the phase eiθn. No force acts on the particle. Yet the sum over sectors gives energy levels that move with θ and cross at θ = π.

How to use this room
  1. Part 1: move θ. Watch the small arrows (one per sector). At θ = 0 all arrows point right. At θ = π the arrows of odd sectors point left. Then change β and see which sectors are heavy.
  2. Part 2: look at the big number. Two completely different sums give the same Z. Move β to a small and to a large value, and watch which sum needs more terms.
  3. Part 3: move θ to π. The two lowest energy levels cross. Part 4: press Run and watch a computer count the windings of random paths.

Part 1 · Paths sort into winding sectors

The picture on the left "unrolls" the ring. The vertical axis is the angle φ, and it keeps growing when the particle goes round. A path from φ = 0 can end at 0, 2π, 4π, or −2π. All these end points are the same place on the ring. Each end point is one sector n. The right picture shows, for each sector, the weight of its paths, and the phase arrow from the flux.

Part 2 · Add the sectors, or add the energy levels

The same number Z can be found in two ways. Way one: add the sectors, Z = Σn eiθn zn. Way two: add the energy levels, Z = Σk e−βEk with Ek = (k − θ/2π)²/2I. The two sums are not the same term by term. Only the totals are equal (Poisson resummation).

Predict, then test: set β small (about 0.5). Which sum needs only one term? Then set β large (about 25). Which sum needs only a few terms now? What does the orange bar row look like at large β?
What the numbers mean

Z is the trace of e−βH for a free particle on a ring, so it includes the sum over the start point (a factor 2π). The sector n has the weight zn = √(2πI/β) e−2π²In²/β. With this factor the two sums are exactly equal. At large β, Z goes to e−βE₀. At small β, Z goes to √(2πI/β).

"Terms needed" is the smallest m for which the partial sum is within 10−10 of Z (relative). The big number uses all terms that matter (until the next term is below 10−17). The difference you see is rounding error of the computer, not physics.

Part 3 · Energy levels from paths, and the current

The levels Ek(θ) are parabolas. They cross at θ = π (and at every odd multiple of π). The free energy −(1/β) ln Z is computed from the sectors only. At large β it follows the lowest parabola. The slope of the lowest level gives a current that flows round the ring, although no force acts.

interpretationThe current is a "ring response to flux". A real metal ring, or a superconducting ring, behaves in a similar way, but only with many particles and more physics.
Predict, then test: move θ from 0 to 2π slowly. At which θ does the current jump? How big is the jump? What happens to the jump when β becomes small?

Part 4 · A computer counts the windings

A Monte Carlo run makes random closed paths on a ring with N time slices, and counts their winding number n. The fraction of paths with each n must follow the weights zn. The flux does not enter the run. It is only used afterwards, to measure ⟨cos θn⟩ = Z(θ)/Z(0).

How often each winding number n appears

What the run measures

simulationbars (orange) and numbers: Metropolis sampling of closed paths on a ring with N slices. It moves one slice at a time, and it also tilts the whole path by one turn (a move that changes n). The error is a block error (20 blocks).
exactblue frames and "exact" numbers: p(n) = zn/Σ zm, ⟨n²⟩ = Σ n² p(n), and ⟨cos θn⟩ = Z(θ)/Z(0).
interpretation⟨n²⟩ is the "topological susceptibility" of the ring. It tells how much the winding number fluctuates.
Predict, then test: at β/I = 6, how often does the path wind once (n = ±1)? Run for some seconds and compare with the blue frames. Then move θ: do the bars change? Does the cos θn number change?
What the numbers mean

The run is a real simulation, so it has a statistical error. Use the "± σ" numbers: the exact value should be inside about two or three σ most of the time. For small β/I (below about 1.5) the winding paths are so rare that a short run never sees one. The page then shows 0 and the exact value is tiny.

Each time slice has the exact short-time weight on the ring (the sum over images), so the only approximation is the finite run length. N slices are chosen so that one step stays small compared with π (N ≈ 6β/I, at least 32).

Guide: what you are looking at

In one minute

Take a particle that can only move on a ring. Think of all paths that start at one point and end at the same point. Some paths do not go round. Some go round once, some twice, some the other way. The number of turns n is the winding number. A path cannot change n by small wiggles, so the paths form sectors.

Now put a thin magnet through the ring. The particle never touches the magnetic field. But its wave gets a phase eiθn for every turn, where θ is set by the flux. This is the effect of Aharonov and Bohm, on a ring. The sum over all paths now has a sector sum with phases.

The sum has a second form: a sum over energy levels, and the flux moves the levels. The two forms are equal. This room lets you see both, and a computer simulation that counts the windings.

Things to try

  1. Part 1: set θ = 0, then θ = π.
    What happens and whyAt θ = 0 every arrow points right: all sectors add. At θ = π the arrow of sector n points at angle πn, so the odd sectors point left and their signed lines are negative. Sector n = ±1 now takes away from Z. Z is smaller at θ = π than at θ = 0.
  2. Part 1: make β small, then large.
    What happens and whyThe weight of sector n is e−2π²In²/β. At small β a winding path must go far in a short time, so it costs a lot, and only n = 0 is left. At large β there is a long time to go round, so many sectors have a weight close to 1. The paths of a large n look like straight lines.
  3. Part 2: β ≈ 0.5, then β ≈ 25. Look at the error plot.
    What happens and whyAt small β the orange curve (sectors) drops below 10−10 with the first term alone (n = 0), and the blue curve (levels) is slow. At large β it is the other way. This is the Poisson duality: the same Z, written as a sum that converges fast in the low-temperature region, or in the high-temperature region.
  4. Part 2: look at the "terms needed" numbers while you move β.
    What happens and whyThe number of sectors needed grows like √β. The number of levels needed grows like 1/√β. The two counts are equal near β = 2πI. The sum with fewer terms is always the best choice.
  5. Part 3: move θ to π.
    What happens and whyThe two lowest parabolas, k = 0 and k = 1, cross. The lowest level E0 has a corner. The curve −(1/β) ln Z is smooth at small β. At large β it follows the corner, but it stays lower by ln 2/β, because two levels count at the crossing. The current jumps by 1/(2πI).
  6. Part 3: look at the current at small and at large β.
    What happens and whyAt large β the current is the zero-temperature sawtooth. At small β the current is small and smooth, because many levels share the weight and the thermal average washes out the jump.
  7. Part 4: set β = 8, I = 1, press Run, and wait.
    What happens and whyThe orange bars grow into the blue frames, and ⟨n²⟩ moves to the exact value (about 0.145). The one-term estimate 2e−2π²I/β = 0.17 is 17 % too high here, because it leaves out the normalisation. The "σ" number gets smaller, like 1/√(run length). Change θ: the bars do not change, because the run does not know θ. Only the cos θn number changes. It is the ratio Z(θ)/Z(0).

The physics

A free particle on a ring has the energy levels Ek = k²/(2I) with k = 0, ±1, ±2, … (ħ = 1, I is the moment of inertia). A flux through the ring shifts the angular momentum: k becomes k − θ/2π. This shift is a pure phase effect. The vector potential is nonzero on the ring, but it is a pure gauge on each small piece. It cannot be removed on the whole ring, because the ring is not simply connected. The group of phases is U(1), and θ is the angle of that group element for one turn.

In the path integral the particle takes all paths. On a ring the paths from φ₀ to φ₁ fall into classes: a path with n turns ends at φ₁ + 2πn when you follow it on the unrolled line. All paths of class n have the same phase from the flux, eiθn, because the flux phase is ∮A·dx = θn. So the propagator on the ring is a sum over the classes of the propagator on the line, with this phase. The sum over classes is the method of images again, as in the box.

For the partition function we use imaginary time β (see Wick rotation). The closed paths are random walks, and their weight is a Gaussian in the number of turns. The sum over n is a Gaussian sum with phases. By the Poisson resummation formula it is a sum of Gaussians in the dual variable k, and the exponent turns into β(k − θ/2π)²/2I. This is the energy sum. So the path sum "knows" the energy levels.

The same story appears in other places. In a periodic potential, the paths that tunnel from one minimum to the next are instantons. Each instanton is one more "turn", and the Bloch angle of the wave gives it the phase eiθ, exactly like the flux here. (The room Tunnelling in Imaginary Time shows instantons in a double well.). In a gauge theory such as QCD, the θ angle is a free parameter, and the sectors are labelled by an integer winding number. In a superconducting ring, the winding number of the phase of the order parameter is fixed, and the flux is quantised in units of the flux quantum.

Honest limits

The particle is free and alone, on a perfect ring with no width, no spin and no potential. A real ring (a mesoscopic metal ring, a superconducting ring) has many electrons, disorder and a finite thickness, and the observed currents are much smaller. The figures with θ are for an ideal flux through the ring; the electron never meets the field. The paths in Part 1 are a sample of three per sector, which is a cartoon of the real sum. The energy levels are exact. The Monte Carlo is a real simulation, so it has a statistical error and it is slow for β/I below about 1.5. A small cosine potential (which turns the crossings into gaps and gives energy bands) is not in this room.
