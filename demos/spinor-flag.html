The Spinor Flag

Turn an electron once around (360°). It is not back where it started. Turn it twice (720°), and it is. Here are four views of the same turn.

How to use this room
  1. Press Play 0 → 720°. Watch all four pictures at once. Look for what is the same at 360° and what is not.
  2. Press 360°. Drag the untangle the belt slider. A message tells you if the box had to move. Then press 720° and drag it again.
  3. Drag with the mouse on the belt or on the sphere to look from another side. Move the turn of the box θ slider by hand to stop at any angle.

What the numbers mean

A rotation by angle θ about the axis n acts on a spin-½ state with the 2×2 matrix U = cos(θ/2)·1 − i sin(θ/2)·(n·σ). The half angle is the key. At θ = 360° the matrix is −1; only at θ = 720° is it +1. The group of all such matrices is SU(2). It is a 3-sphere, and it covers the ordinary rotations twice.

The belt shows the same fact with ordinary objects. A path of rotations from "no turn" to "one turn" (360°) cannot be pulled flat. A path to "two turns" (720°) can. The untangle slider uses the deformation R(s,u) = rot(x, θs/2) · rot(b(u), θs/2), where the axis b swings from +x to −x. At 720° the end of the belt stays still for every u. At 360° it cannot.

The flag follows Penrose: the pole is ψ†σψ, the flag is the real part of ψᵀ(iσy)σψ. A phase eiχ turns the flag by 2χ, so the sign −1 turns it by 360° and is invisible.

Interference: the output brightness is I = (1 + Re⟨ψ|U|ψ⟩)/2 = (1 + cos(θ/2))/2. Neutron experiments in 1975 (Rauch et al.; Werner et al.) saw this 720° period.

Guide: what you are looking at

In one minute

Turn a ball by 360° (one full turn) and it is back where it started. An electron is different. Its quantum state is multiplied by −1 after one full turn. Only after 720° (two full turns) is the state the same as before.

You cannot see a minus sign on a single electron, because the probabilities do not change. But you can see it if you split a beam of electrons (or neutrons), turn the spin in one arm only, and join the beams again. The two arms then add up or cancel. The picture repeats after 720°, not after 360°. Physicists did this with neutrons in 1975.

The belt is an ordinary-object picture of the same fact. A belt from a wall to a box, with the box turned by one full turn, has a twist you cannot remove without moving the box. With two full turns, you can remove the twist. The four panels show this from four sides.

Things to try

  1. Set 360° and move untangle. Can the belt get flat while the box stays still? Then try 720°.
    What happens and whyAt 360° it cannot. While you drag the slider, the red message shows that the box has to turn away from its start orientation (by up to 180° in the middle of the slider). That is not allowed: the box must stay still. At 720° the box never moves for any setting of the slider, and the belt becomes flat. So one full turn leaves a twist that you cannot remove. Two full turns do not. A path of rotations that goes once round cannot be shrunk to nothing. A path that goes twice round can.
  2. Press Play and watch panels 2 and 3 together.
    What happens and whyAt 360° the spin and the flag in panel 2 are back at their start. But the two arrows in panel 3 point the other way, and the circle in the bottom has the dot at −1. The pole and the flag cannot see the minus sign. The state does. At 720° everything is back.
  3. Set 180° and then 360° in panel 4.
    What happens and whyAt 180° the brightness is 0.5. At 360° it is 0. A neutron whose spin is turned by 360° in one arm cancels the beam of the other arm. A normal object would give full brightness at 360°. This is the experiment: the sign −1 is real, because it is a relative sign between two arms.
  4. Drag the mouse on the belt.
    What happens and whyYou see the belt from other sides. The orange and blue plates show how each plate is turned. The twist is a property of the whole belt. It does not depend on the view.

The physics

An electron has spin ½. Its state is a spinor: two complex numbers. A rotation acts on a spinor with a 2 × 2 matrix. The set of these matrices is the Lie group SU(2). It is a three-dimensional sphere. Every ordinary rotation in space is covered twice by SU(2): the matrices U and −U give the same rotation. A path from +1 to −1 in SU(2) is a closed path of ordinary rotations (one full turn) but not a closed path of matrices.

The direction of the spin is a point on a sphere, the Bloch sphere. It comes back after 360°. The spinor itself does not. A global sign changes no probability, so one electron alone cannot show it. Only a comparison with a part that was not turned can. That is what the interferometer does. The same idea is behind the fact that particles with half-integer spin (fermions) behave differently from particles with integer spin.

The same kind of group appears in particle physics: SU(2) in the weak force (a different symmetry, not a turn of space) and SU(3) in the strong force. The room Three Colours shows what SU(3) does. The room Order Matters shows another feature of spin: rotations about different axes do not commute.

The math

Show the formulas
U(n, θ) = e−iθ n·σ/2 = cos(θ/2) · 1 − i sin(θ/2) · (n·σ) The rotation of a spin-½ state by angle θ about the axis n. The σ are the three 2 × 2 Pauli matrices. The half angle is the key. U(n, 2π) = −1,   U(n, 4π) = +1 One full turn gives −1. Two full turns give +1. ψ = (a, b),   s = ψ†σψ = (2 Re a*b,  2 Im a*b,  |a|² − |b|²) The direction of the spin (the pole). Replace ψ by −ψ and s does not change, because the minus sign cancels. ψ(θ) = U(x, θ) ψ(0) In the room, the start state points along +y, and the turn is about the x axis. The pole then goes round a circle in the y–z plane. I(θ) = ½ (1 + Re⟨ψ|U|ψ⟩) = ½ (1 + cos(θ/2)) The brightness of the interferometer. The beams add as ½(ψ + Uψ), and I is its squared length. The result has period 720°, not 360°. R(s, u) = rot(x, θs/2) · rot(b(u), θs/2),   b(u) = (cos πu, sin πu, 0) The belt in the room: s runs from 0 (wall) to 1 (box) and u is the untangle slider. The axis b swings from +x to −x. At θ = 720° the end s = 1 stays at the identity for every u. At θ = 360° the end moves, and so it is not allowed. SU(2) → SO(3),   2 to 1;   π1(SO(3)) = ℤ2 The topology behind the belt. A loop of rotations that goes once round cannot be shrunk. Going twice round can.

Honest limits

All four panels use one idealised spin-½ state, with no noise and no environment. The belt is a way to draw a path of rotations. It is not a real belt with thickness and friction. The "flag" follows Penrose's picture and is a visual aid. The interferometer is an ideal one: a real neutron experiment (Rauch et al., Werner et al., 1975) turns the spin in a magnetic field and sees the same 720° period, but with imperfect contrast. The "untangle" slider is a demonstration of why the twist stays at 360°. It is not a proof. The proof is the topology in the last formula. At 360° and slider 1, the belt looks flat and the box is back at its start; but the box had to swing away for the settings in between.
